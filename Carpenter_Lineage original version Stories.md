**A companion to *The Carpenter Lineage: A Documented History of the Family of James Edmund Carpenter II*.** Every story in this volume is drawn from the documented register of the main work — every claim carries the evidence grade the main work assigns it, every quote is on paper, and every legend is marked where it stands. Where this book says a thing happened, the main book says *how we know*. Nothing here is invented; where the record runs out, this book says so.

**How the relations in this book are counted:** one generation up is a parent; two, a grandparent; three, a great-grandparent; four and beyond, numbered great-grandparents (the fourth generation up is a second great-grandparent, and so on). The deep register spans roughly forty-five documented generations from the birth of 1980. Ordinals marked "approximately" ride on the chain as documented in the main work; collateral kin and kin-by-marriage are labeled as such, never blurred into the direct line.

# PART ONE — THE DEEP REGISTER

## I. The Two Saints of Metz (c. 580–640)

**Relation to James Edmund Carpenter II: direct ancestors — Saint Arnulf by the father-line and Saint Pepin of Landen by the mother-line of the same marriage; approximately the forty-third great-grandfathers. The oldest documented names in the line.**

To understand why a family's oldest graves are a pair of saints, you have to see the world they ruled — the strangest kingdom in Europe.

The Merovingian kings of the Franks were still on the throne in 580, and they were still, in theory, the heirs of Clovis, the conqueror who had made one kingdom out of the Roman ruins of Gaul. But by the sixth century the dynasty had rotted into what later historians called the *rois fainéants* — the do-nothing kings. They were kept in their palaces like relics themselves: long-haired, ceremonially carried about, consulted on omens. One king, Theuderic III, is remembered in the chronicle for weeping in his saddle after a defeat, too broken even to command. The real power in the kingdom had drained, quietly and permanently, into the office held by the greatest man of the aristocracy: the *maior domus*, the mayor of the palace — master of the household, controller of the treasury, the army, and the government.

Two men held that power in the generation this family's record begins with.

**The first was Arnulf.** He was born around 582, into the Austrasian aristocracy of the Rhine and Moselle, and he was given the Church's greatest see in the east: the bishopric of Metz. Arnulf was not a cloistered bishop. The letters and lives that survive him show a man in the middle of every great event of his age — counselor of kings, judge of bishops, general of armies. When the eastern kingdom went to war, Arnulf went with it; when the court feuded, Arnulf was the man both sides tried to keep. He had married before taking holy orders — his wife is remembered as Doda — and they had at least two sons. And then, late in life, he did the thing that made him a saint in the eyes of the age: he resigned his see, surrendered his wealth, and withdrew to a monastery in the mountains near Remiremont, where he lived as a simple monk until his death around 640. The faithful began to venerate his tomb almost immediately. Miracles were reported. The church counted him a confessor-saint — Saint Arnulf of Metz.

**The second was Pepin.** Pepin of Landen — "Pepin the Old" — was the mayor of the palace of Austrasia, the lay counterpart of Arnulf, and the richest landowner in the kingdom. His story has one dark, famous chapter. The kingdom was then effectively ruled from the west by the old queen Brunhilda — Brunhilda of the Visigoths, wife of a dead king, regent for her great-grandsons, and by the chronicles' unanimous account a tyrant of reptilian tenacity, governing through torture and vendetta. In 613 the great men of Austrasia — and Pepin of Landen was first among them — rose and gave Brunhilda to her enemies. She was executed with a cruelty the chronicle records without flinching: broken on the wheel, tied to the tails of untamed horses, dragged to death. Pepin emerged from that revolution as the most powerful subject in Francia. He died about 640, and like Arnulf, he was venerated as a saint — Saint Pepin of Landen.

Here is the hinge of everything that follows — the single fact the whole early Middle Ages turns on, and the fact this family's line turns on with it. **Pepin of Landen's daughter, Saint Begga, married Arnulf of Metz's son, Ansegisel.** The wedding joined the two great houses of Austrasia — the lay power and the episcopal power, the sword and the crozier — into one blood. Their sons were Pepin of Herstal, the first man to rule all Francia in fact; his son was Charles Martel, the hammer of the Saracens at Tours; his son was Pepin the Short, who took the crown itself and gave it to the Carolingian line; and his son was Charlemagne.

The saints had one tragedy of their own. Ansegisel — Begga's husband, Arnulf's son — was murdered in a feud, killed by a rival nobleman in the vendetta culture of the age. Begga buried him and lived on as one of the great pious widows of the dynasty — a founder of churches, a saint in her own right. The feud that killed her husband was the dark side of the aristocratic world the family descends from: these were not tranquil abbots but war-lords who happened to end as saints.

*What the record holds: both men are documented by near-contemporary lives; the marriage of Ansegisel and Begga is genealogical bedrock; the 613 overthrow of Brunhilda is in the chronicles; the cults are real. What the record refuses: the pedigree that made Arnulf a grandson of Merovingian royalty — a fabrication of Charlemagne's own court historians, rejected by modern scholarship. So the family's oldest ancestors are not kings, but two mayors of the palace — the men who actually ran the kingdom — canonized for how they ended. The chain begins there, at c. 580, honestly, and calls it the floor.*

## II. The Emperor and the Boy He Failed (814)

**Relation: Charlemagne — approximately the fortieth great-grandfather, through King Pepin of Italy and Bernard; Bernard of Italy — approximately the thirty-ninth great-grandfather, the blighted young king of this story. The line runs through Bernard's infant son, spared.**

Charlemagne was the greatest man in Europe, and at the end of his life he buried his children one by one.

The family descends from his son **Pepin**, whom the emperor made **King of Italy** — crowned by his father's will, ruling the whole Italian peninsula from Ravenna, recognized by the pope in Rome. Pepin was capable and dutiful, the sort of son an emperor can use. He died in the summer of 810, at about thirty-three years old, leaving the Italian kingdom to his own son — **Bernard**, a boy of eight.

Charlemagne died on 28 January 814, worn out at seventy-two, after a reign of forty-six years. The succession had been settled long before, and it did not include Bernard. The empire — all of it, from the Spanish March to the Elbe — passed to Charlemagne's surviving legitimate son, **Louis**, called the Pious: a serious, anxious, deeply religious man who had spent his adult life ruling the sub-kingdom of Aquitaine in his father's shadow and had come to fear his own family as his father never did.

Louis suspected his dead brother's son almost at once. Bernard, now twenty-four and King of Italy, was summoned from Ravenna under assurances of safe conduct — he came, by one account, on the promise of his uncle's protection. At the assembly his enemies accused him of treason: of plotting to make himself emperor, or of scheming to keep Italy by force. The sources never settle what, if anything, Bernard actually planned. What they settle is what followed. The young king was condemned to the traditional Frankish punishment for treason: **blinding**. The sentence was carried out at Chalon-sur-Saône in the spring of 817.

Two days later, Bernard was dead — of the wounds to his eyes, or, the chroniclers add, of something worse: the young king refused food after the blinding, and starved himself to death in his cell. He was twenty-four.

The consequences ran through the rest of Louis's reign. The emperor did public penance for the death — first privately, then, in 822, before the assembled army at Attigny in one of the most extraordinary scenes of the century: the anointed ruler of Europe prostrate before his own host, confessing the death of his nephew. The chroniclers say he was never at peace again. Bernard's blighting became the wound at the center of Louis's troubled reign — and his reign fell apart into civil war among his own sons, the empire partitioned at Verdun in 843 into the kingdoms that became France and Germany.

And Bernard's blood survived — in the one place no one had thought to look. He left a baby son, **Pepin**, spared because an infant could not threaten anyone. That boy was raised in obscurity, married, and founded the line of the **Counts of Vermandois** — the line the family's medieval register follows down the centuries: Vermandois, Leicester, Quincy, la Zouche, Holland, to the Hawkes and the Carpenters. The broken Italian branch, blighted at twenty-four, rescued by an infant — that is the Carolingian line the family descends from.

*Every stage is documented: the succession, the blinding at Chalon, the death, the penance at Attigny, the survival of the son. The register carries it at [PR] through the royal annals and the modern scholarly reconstruction.*

## III. The Ward of the King (1181) and the Son Who Named His Mother (1979)

**Relation: Ida de Tosny — approximately the twenty-fourth great-grandmother; her son William Longespée — approximately the twenty-fourth great-grandfather. The royal gateway: Henry II is the twenty-fifth great-grandfather through them.**

The greatest story in this register is a mystery solved by a single Latin sentence, 762 years after the fact.

**Ida de Tosny** was born into one of the half-dozen greatest baronial houses of England and Normandy. The Tosnys were old conquerors' blood — her ancestor Roger de Tosny had been the banner-bearer of Normandy at Hastings, in the front rank of the charge at Senlac Hill. By the 1170s the family held great estates on both sides of the Channel, and Ida, as the heiress of the line, was a ward of the crown. The king was **Henry II** — in his fifties, the most powerful man in Europe, and a man who had never in his life regarded a beautiful ward as anything but his own business. Ida became the king's mistress. She bore him a son.

The boy was perhaps two when the king had to settle her future, and a royal mistress who was also a great heiress could not be left unwed. At the Christmas court of 1181, Henry gave Ida to **Roger Bigod, Earl of Norfolk** — a hard, able, twenty-something soldier who had fought for the king in the Young King's rebellion and earned his way back into favor. With the bride, the king restored three manors as her marriage portion. It was a political wedding in the king's own hall, and the bride had a two-year-old son by the man giving her away.

The boy grew up as **William Longespée** — "Longsword," after a famous ancestor. Illegitimate sons of kings in that age were not hidden; they were used. William was raised at court, knighted young, and proved himself on campaign in France. Then came the making of him: the heiress **Ela, Countess of Salisbury** — one of the greatest inheritances in England — was given to him in marriage, and through her he became **Earl of Salisbury**, with the sword of state carried before him. He served Richard the Lionheart in France; he served King John — long enough to be one of the few earls who stood by John through the worst of it — and then the boy Henry III, carrying the royal standard at the rout of Damme in Flanders. He died at his own castle of Salisbury in March 1226. The rumor of the court was poison — a servant, William de Button, hanged for it later — and the truth is lost. He lies under an effigy in Salisbury Cathedral, the figure in mail with a sword, one of the finest military tombs in England; when the tomb was opened in the eighteenth century the antiquaries recorded a body of unusual size.

And his mother? The chroniclers of the time — who knew perfectly well who the young earl's father was — never wrote her name down plainly. For seven centuries, antiquaries argued: was it Ida de Tosny, or another of the king's women? The question sat open from 1226 to the twentieth century.

Then, in **1979**, a charter was found in the archives. It was a grant made by the earl himself, witnessed in his own court, and in it he speaks of the woman concerned as ***"Comitissa Ida, mater mea."* — "Countess Ida, my mother."**

The boy given away at Christmas had carried his mother's name in his own documents for his whole life. The paper had been there since 1226. It had simply taken 762 years for someone to read the right page. Through William and Ela — through their daughter **Ela Longespée** — the family's royal gateway runs: son of a king, grandson of the banner-bearer of Hastings, named by his own hand.

## IV. The Saint the Conqueror Executed (1054–1076)

**Relation: Saint Waltheof — approximately the thirtieth great-grandfather, through his daughter, who married into the Tosny house and carried the line down thirteen generations to Ida de Tosny. Judith of Lens, his wife — the Conqueror's niece — is the same generation.**

**Waltheof** was born about 1050 into the strangest inheritance in England — the two oldest warrior stocks in the island fused in one boy. His father was **Siward the Dane**, Earl of Northumbria: the giant of the sagas, so tall a special doorway was cut for him, the man who had defeated Macbeth at Dunsinane in 1054 — yes, that Macbeth — and ruled the north for decades. His mother was of the ancient house of Bamburgh, the Anglo-Danish dynasty that had held Northumbria since King Eadwulf in 913. When Siward died in 1055, the boy Waltheof inherited the claim of both lines.

He came of age in catastrophe. In 1069 the north rose against the Conqueror, and William answered with the Harrying — the deliberate devastation of Yorkshire, a scorched-earth winter in which the chroniclers say the survivors were reduced to eating horses, dogs, and one another. Waltheof, young and alive and too dangerous to ignore, submitted to William — and the Conqueror, who understood the value of legitimacy, made him Earl of Northumbria and then, after the earl of Huntingdon's death, gave him that earldom too. And in 1070 the Conqueror did something more: he married Waltheof to his own niece, **Judith of Lens** — daughter of his sister Adelaide.

In that marriage bed, the family's two deepest threads became one flesh. The groom carried the blood of the Bamburgh kings, of the Dane who broke Macbeth, of the saints and earls of the old North. The bride carried the blood of William himself. The register's two oldest lines — the Anglo-Danish and the Norman — met on their wedding night, and never parted.

Six years later came the fall. In 1075 the earls of Hereford and Norfolk raised the last revolt of the Conqueror's reign — the Revolt of the Earls. Waltheof was drawn in; how far, the sources argue over to this day. He knew of the plot, and wavered, and in the end did what his conscience told him: he went to the Archbishop of Canterbury, Lanfranc, and confessed the whole thing, and did penance, and threw himself on the king's mercy.

The king had no mercy to give. Waltheof was arrested, held at Winchester, and in the spring of 1076 brought out for execution on St Giles's Hill, outside the city walls. He was, the chroniclers all say, a man of extraordinary beauty, and the scene undid the executioners: their first blows missed, or turned, and it took several strokes to end him. He was twenty-five or twenty-six. His body was thrown into a ditch outside the walls — the Conqueror had forbidden it burial.

But the monks of Crowland Abbey, who had loved him — who said he had come to their fenland church and prayed there like a simple man — came by night, secretly, and took the body up. And here the chronicle becomes hagiography, and the file carries it as hagiography, marked: the monks said the body was whole and sweet, incorrupt, after days in the ditch. They buried him in their church. Miracles were reported at the grave. The church made him a saint — **Saint Waltheof of Crowland** — and a Norse poet of the age composed his memorial, the *Valþjófsflokkr*, one of the last poems of the old North. His daughter, through Judith, carried the line on — and through her the line runs, forty-odd documented generations later, to this family.

The Conqueror, who had married him to his own blood, executed him and dumped him in a ditch. The monks rescued the body and made him a saint. The niece who married him carried his blood into every generation that followed. He is the family's martyr — and he is, in the precise sense, family.

## V. Alfred in the Marshes (878)

**Relation: Alfred the Great — approximately the thirty-eighth great-grandfather, through the West Saxon kings to the marriage of Saint Waltheof and Judith.**

The year that nearly ended England began with a Christmas feast.

Twelfth Night, 878. **King Alfred of Wessex**, thirty years old, the greatest of the West Saxon kings, is holding the feast at Chippenham in Wiltshire — the old royal estate, deep in his heartland. The court is at table. The guards are few; it is deep winter; the war has been quiet.

The war had not been quiet. It had been silent and patient. The Danish Great Heathen Army — the same host that had destroyed two other English kingdoms — had slipped into Wessex over the winter, and on that Twelfth Night it struck the feast itself. Alfred fought his way out of the hall and into the dark with what men he could gather. By morning the royal estate was in Danish hands, the kingdom's army was scattered or dead, and the king of Wessex was a fugitive with a price on his head.

He fled south and west, into the country no army could take: the Somerset Levels, a drowned landscape of reeds and black water, where the only dry ground was the islands. On one of these — **Athelney**, a few acres of firm ground in the marshes — Alfred hid through the hardest winter of his life. A later legend says that he sheltered in a poor man's hut, was asked to watch the cakes baking on the hearth, forgot them in his worry, and was roundly scolded by the peasant's wife who did not know him. It is the most famous story in English history. It is also, this register insists, a legend — first told two hundred years later — and it is told here only so the reader knows the difference.

What the documents hold is better, because it is true. Asser, the Welsh scholar who knew Alfred and wrote his life, says the king had almost no one left — a few thegns, some of his household. Through the winter he moved among the marsh-islands, and the people of Somerset who had not submitted came to him secretly, and he planned. And in the Easter season of 878 he came out of the marshes to an ancient meeting-stone called **Egbert's Stone**, where the fighting men of Somerset, Wiltshire, and Hampshire had gathered to him — men who had held to their king through the occupation and told him they would live or die with him now. A week later, at a place called **Edington**, Alfred met the Danish army under its king Guthrum in the open field — and broke it completely. The rout ran all night.

The settlement that followed is the birth certificate of England. Guthrum sued for peace, and the peace had a price the sagas would have understood: the Viking king accepted **baptism**, and **Alfred stood as his godfather** — the two kings who had tried to destroy each other bound in the font, with Alfred giving his defeated enemy the Christian name of the West Saxon royal house, Æthelstan. The treaty divided England down the middle: the Danelaw to Guthrum, Wessex and the south to Alfred. Within a generation the boundary would fall; within two, England would be one kingdom, under Alfred's own house.

Alfred rebuilt everything in the years left to him: a fort in every shire, a fleet, a law code, schools, and translations of the books he thought every free man should read — translating Boethius himself, in the midst of government. He was, his own words say, a man who had longed for learning in his youth and been denied it, and he spent his kingship making sure others were not denied.

The family descends from his son Edward, through the old West Saxon kings, through Edgar and Æthelred, to the marriage of the saint and the Conqueror's niece — and the honest floor stands at **Ingild, 718**, Alfred's ancestor, above which the paper runs out and the register says so. From the marsh at Athelney to the last name on the list: one king's winter of despair, one army's muster at a stone, and forty-five generations of documented descent.

## VI. The Two Crusades and the Charter (1190–1219)

**Relation: Saher de Quincy — approximately the twenty-sixth great-grandfather (through his granddaughter Elena, who married the Justiciar Alan la Zouche); Alan la Zouche — approximately the twenty-fifth great-grandfather; William Malet — approximately the twenty-seventh great-grandfather (through his daughter Mabel, who married Hugh de Vivonne); Hugh de Vivonne — the same generation, approximately the twenty-seventh great-grandfather. Margaret of Leicester, Saher's wife and the Leicester heiress — approximately the twenty-sixth great-grandmother.**

Two grandfathers of this line, one on each side of the Magna Carta story, took the cross in the two great crusades that frame the age.

**The first: William Malet and the Lionheart's crusade, 1190.** William Malet, lord of Curry Malet in Somerset — the family's other Magna Carta line — sailed with **Richard the Lionheart** on the Third Crusade. The expedition was the wonder of the age: Richard took Cyprus on the way, won the siege of Acre, broke Saladin's army at Arsuf, and came within sight of Jerusalem twice — and turned back twice, judging he could not hold the city even if he took it. The crusade ended in a truce: the coast to the Christians, Jerusalem to Saladin, pilgrim access guaranteed. William Malet came home with the army — and the register remembers him as a crusader for the rest of his life.

Twenty-five years later, an old man, he did the thing for which history knows him. In June 1215, at Runnymede, **William Malet put his name among the twenty-five barons** who were to force King John to keep the Great Charter. He was one of the oldest men on the list. He did not live to see what the Charter became: he died in **December 1215**, within six months of the sealing.

And here the patent rolls preserve one of the great ironies of the whole story. On **20 December 1215 — six months to the day after Runnymede** — King John granted the confiscated Malet estates to a loyal Poitevin knight, **Hugh de Vivonne**, who had stayed true through the rebellion. The rebel's lands, forfeited, to the king's man: that was the logic of the civil war. But the ending belongs to the family. The rebellion was settled, the excommunications lifted, the peace made — and before November 1223, **Hugh de Vivonne married the rebel's daughter and heiress, Mabel Malet.** The king's loyalist and the baron's blood were joined; the lands came home through the marriage; and the family descends from that wedding. Rebellion and loyalty, reconciled in one generation.

**The second: Saher de Quincy and the road to Damietta, 1215–1219.** Saher de Quincy, Earl of Winchester, was the hardest of the three sureties in this register. He was made earl only in 1207 — through his wife **Margaret of Leicester**, sister and co-heiress of the great earldom — and he was a man who treated his word as a load-bearing wall. In 1215 he put his name to the barons' list, and when the Pope annulled the Charter and excommunicated the barons for enforcing it, Saher enforced it anyway: the register carries that he was excommunicated by name. When civil war came, he went the whole way — he sailed with Robert FitzWalter to France and helped offer the English crown to Prince Louis, the king of France's son. For a year and more England had two kings.

Defeat came in 1217: the French beaten at the Battle of Lincoln, their fleet destroyed at Sandwich, Louis bought off by treaty. Saher submitted and was restored — and did what men of his world did with a ruined old age: he took the cross.

The Fifth Crusade gathered at the Nile Delta in 1218 against **Damietta**, the great port of Egypt — the key, the strategists believed, to Jerusalem itself. The siege was a thing out of the chronicles' wildest pages: two siege towers, five stories tall, floated against the walls on ships; the defenders stretched a great chain across the Nile; fire-ships came down the current at the crusaders' engines; and through it all the camp sickness worked — scurvy, then dysentery, killing more men than the arrows did. The chronicle of Oliver of Paderborn, who was there, records the siege tower that ran aground and the knight who fought alone on its deck, and the legate preaching on the ashes.

Saher de Quincy, Earl of Winchester, an Englishman in his sixties, in a land he had no reason to be in, sick in a camp of sickness, held on with the host for eighteen months. The city surrendered on **5 November 1219**. **Saher de Quincy died there on 3 November 1219 — two days before the victory.**

His body was buried at Acre, the crusader city on the Syrian coast. His heart was carried home across the whole length of Europe and buried at **Garendon Abbey in Leicestershire** — the county of his wife's inheritance, the Leicester co-heiress through whom he had become earl in the first place. The body in the crusader kingdom; the heart in Leicestershire. His son Roger carried the earldom on, and Roger's daughter **Elena de Quincy** married **Alan la Zouche**, the Justiciar of England — the marriage from which the family descends.

The crusade he died on marched on to Cairo two years later, was trapped by the Nile flood, and surrendered everything — Damietta included. That disaster he missed. The Charter he had been excommunicated for enforcing was reissued in his grandsons' time, confirmed, reconfirmed, and entered into the law of England, where it stands today. The crusade is a footnote. The Charter is not. The family descends from the man who chose the Charter.

## VII. Brian Boru Wins and Dies in the Same Hour (1014)

**Relation: Brian Boru — approximately the thirtieth great-grandfather, through his descendant Aoife, the Clare marriage, the Marshals and the Vivonnes, to the same Joan de Vivonne from whom the line descends.**

**Brian Boru** was seventy years old on Good Friday of 1014, and he had spent fifty years making himself king of everything.

He was born about 941 into the **Dál Cais**, a proud but second-rank dynasty of the lower Shannon — not the old high-kingly line of the Uí Néill, but a family of fighters on the way up. His brother Mahon was murdered by the Norse of Limerick; Brian avenged him, took Limerick, and then spent the rest of his life doing one thing, over and over, better than anyone in Ireland: winning. The annals credit him with fifty battles. He broke the Norse towns, broke the Uí Néill, broke every provincial king who stood against him, and in 1002 took the high kingship itself — the first man outside the old line ever to hold it. He exacted tribute from the whole island. The annals called him emperor of the Irish.

In 1013 the revolt came. **Sigtrygg Silkbeard**, Norse king of Dublin — technically Brian's subordinate — gave refuge to Brian's rebellious son-in-law and defied the high king. Brian, at seventy, gathered the levies of half of Ireland and laid siege to Dublin through the winter. On the eve of Easter 1014, the two armies drew up outside the city, on the field of **Clontarf**, by the sea.

The sagas fill the eve with omens — a hag washing bloody garments in a ford, the blessed maiden who walked the camps — and the register keeps them as saga, marked. What the annals carry is battle, hour by hour. It raged all day. Brian's son **Murchad** — his best soldier, his heir — fought in the front rank and broke the enemy line, and was killed in the winning of it. The Norse-Leinster host broke at last and ran for the ships, and the running was a second battle: the tide was out, the longboats were stranded, and the Irish cut the fugitives down on the strand and in the surf.

And in his tent, behind the Irish lines, knelt the old high king — seventy years old, too old for the shield-wall, at his prayers, they said, with his psalter — when a fleeing Norse lord named **Brodir** broke into the tent. Brodir had been a Christian and an apostate, a sorcerer by reputation; he had been told he would live if he did not ride the day of the battle, and he had fought on foot. Now, running from the ruin of his army, he found the king.

He killed Brian Boru in his tent at the hour of his life's victory.

The annals' verdict on the whole thing is the family's epitaph for him: *"Brian, son of Cennétig, overking of the Irish, the Augustus of the whole of north-west Europe, was killed."* His body was carried to Armagh and buried with the kings. He had won everything, and died in the hour of winning, and the dynasty he founded ruled Munster for generations. His blood reached this family through the marriage of his descendant **Aoife** into the house of Clare — Strongbow's line — and through Isabel de Clare and William Marshal down to the Vivonne marriage and the line. The male-line pedigree of his own dynasty is documented back to **Cennétig in 896** — the family's Irish floor. And the O'Briens who bear his name today carry, the DNA project says, his own Y-chromosome — the high king's signature, still readable a thousand years later in the genes of his clan.

## VIII. The Viking Who Took a Kingdom as a Gift (911)

**Relation: Rollo — approximately the thirty-sixth great-grandfather, through his daughter Gerloc (the christened Adele), whose blood ran down fourteen generations to Ida de Tosny.**

The family's oldest state document is a treaty, and the man who sealed it was a pirate.

For a hundred years the Northmen had made the Seine their highway. They sacked Rouen in 841 — the monks of Saint-Denis recorded the horror of it — and in 845 a fleet took Paris and was bought off with seven thousand pounds of silver. Every king of the West Franks knew the pattern: the fleets came up the river every summer, the monasteries burned, the villages paid, and the royal army arrived after the raiders left.

**Rollo** — the family's ancestor through his daughter — emerges from this world, and the register is honest about the sources: everything about his early life comes from **Dudo of Saint-Quentin**, a chronicler writing a century later for Rollo's own descendants, which means the early story is dynastic saga, and it is marked as such. What Dudo tells is irresistible. Rollo, a Norwegian of noble blood, harried the coasts of Flanders and the lower Seine until the French king — Charles the Simple — was advised to buy what he could not beat. In **911**, at the little town of **Saint-Clair-sur-Epte** on the river's northern bend, they made the deal that founded a nation: the land of the lower Seine — the country the Northmen had taken anyway — granted to Rollo and his men as a hereditary principality, in exchange for baptism, loyalty, and a promise that no Viking fleet would ever pass it again. The saga keeps the color and the register flags it: Rollo, at the font, waved away the proposed christening name until his godfather, the Count of Rouen, simply held *him* under the water; and when the ceremony required him to kiss the king's foot in homage, Rollo declined on principle and directed a warrior to do it — and the warrior, a proper Norseman, seized the royal foot and tipped the king backward over his saddle, to the delight of the whole host.

The record proper begins in **918**, with the first surviving document that names him: a charter of King Charles to the abbey of Saint-Denis, in which the river land around Rouen appears as the possession of **"Rolphus"** — Rollo, in the king's own Latin, holding the grant the treaty made. The pirate was now a duke in writing.

He ruled his new land for a decade and more, and he understood his people's future: his son **William Longsword** — the name itself a policy — was sent to be raised at the French court, to learn the language and the faith of the kingdom the Northmen had joined. It nearly killed the dynasty anyway: William was murdered at a peace meeting in 942 by Arnulf of Flanders, a killing the sagas called the foulest of the age. But the graft held. By the next generation the Normans were the most formidable force in Christendom — French-speaking, church-building, feudal Europe's sharpest instrument.

And through it all, the blood threaded outward: Rollo's daughter **Gerloc** — christened Adele at the font — married **William, Count of Poitiers and Duke of Aquitaine**, one of the greatest princes of France. The register carries her, by name, in the direct line: the Viking's own daughter, in this family's ancestry, five generations before the Conquest carried her blood across the Channel. From a pirate's treaty at Saint-Clair-sur-Epte to a dukedom in England in a hundred years — and the family's paper on it begins, as the family insists paper must, with the 918 charter and the name *Rolphus* in the king's own hand.

# PART TWO — THE COLONIAL SHELF

## IX. The Fortune's Gamble (1621)

**Relation: no blood of the Cushmans runs in the line — but Robert Cushman's granddaughter Sarah Cushman married the direct ancestor John Hawkes, and she was herself the granddaughter of Isaac Allerton, who is approximately the thirteenth great-grandfather. Kin by marriage twice over; the wedding that bound them is documented in Thomas Cushman's will of 1690.**

The family's most famous wedding — the marriage that bound the Cushmans to the Allertons and to this line — was nearly lost in the Atlantic.

**Robert Cushman** was the business genius of the Leiden Pilgrims: a grocer's apprentice from Rolvenden in Kent, excommunicated at Canterbury for his Separatism, made free again, and then — as the Leiden congregation's Chief Agent in London — the man who, with John Carver, negotiated the patent, found the ships, and arranged the money that made the Mayflower voyage possible. He was supposed to sail on the Mayflower himself. When the Speedwell failed in the Channel — twice, leaking badly, the whole venture hanging by a thread — Cushman stayed behind to untangle the wreckage of the contracts, and sent his thirteen-year-old son Thomas in his place. The boy crossed with the strangers; the father promised to follow.

He followed on the ***Fortune***, in July 1621 — a ship of fifty-five tons, barely a third the Mayflower's size, carrying about thirty-five passengers and no supplies at all. The crossing took months; when the Fortune anchored off Cape Cod on 9 November 1621, Bradford's account of the landing is one of the great deadpan sentences of the colonial record: the ship's company, stepping ashore at the end of the starving year, found *"not so much as a biscuit cake... nor pot or pan to dress any meat in."* Robert Cushman had arrived in a colony with nothing to feed him.

He stayed two weeks — and in those two weeks he did the three things for which he is remembered. He preached the sermon that has outlived every sermon of the founding generation: on 9 December 1621, standing in the half-built settlement, he delivered *"The Sin and Danger of Self-Love"* — the great exhortation on community — and then, with John Carver's successor Bradford, obtained what the Mayflower's agents had failed to get: the colonists' signatures on the articles of agreement they had refused the year before. The Fortune sailed home December 13 loaded with clapboard and beaver — the colony's first export, five hundred pounds' worth — and on the way was taken by a French warship. Cushman was imprisoned in France for weeks. The signed articles and the only copies of *Mourt's Relation* survived in a chest. Robert Cushman reached England in February 1622, spent three more years as the colony's agent in London — the man who kept the patent and the money flowing — and died there in the spring of 1625, aged forty-eight, before he could ever make Plymouth his home.

His son stayed. **Thomas Cushman** — the boy who had crossed at thirteen — grew up as Governor Bradford's ward, and in 1649 became Ruling Elder of the Plymouth church, the office he held for forty-three years. And he married **Mary Allerton** — the youngest daughter of Isaac Allerton, the last survivor of the Mayflower, dead in 1699 — and their daughter **Sarah Cushman married John Hawkes of Lynn**, the family's direct ancestor. The man who missed the Mayflower, took the Fortune, was captured by the French, and never saw his American home — his blood is in this family through the wedding of his granddaughter to a Hawkes. Thomas Cushman's will of 1690 names her: *"Sarah Hoaks."*

## X. The Night the Dam Broke (1682)

**Relation: John Hawkes of Lynn — the twelfth great-grandfather. The sons he sent to cut the dam, Adam and Moses, are the great-great-grandsons of the line; Moses Hawke, born 1659, is the eleventh great-grandfather.**

The most famous lawsuit in the history of Lynn, Massachusetts, was a family war — and the family won.

The story begins with water. **John Hawkes** of Lynn — the family's direct ancestor, born about 1633, twin brother of Susanna who married William Cogswell — farmed land along the Saugus River, beside the great Iron Works whose dam flooded his meadows whenever the owners let the water build. The works had changed hands; the new owner, **Samuel Appleton**, one of the richest men in the colony, refused to manage the water. In May of 1682, the Hawkes farm flooded again.

John Hawkes did not petition. He did not sue — not first, anyway. What he did, the court record preserves in the language of admiration and outrage: he was, the plaintiffs charged, *"the only plotter, contriver and secret manager of the cutting or breaking of the great dam at Hamersmith."* On a night in May 1682, he sent his sons — Adam and Moses, the register names them, with a neighbor, Joseph Trumbull — to cut the great dam in the dark and let the river run free. The boys did it. The water went. The meadows drained.

Appleton sued. The case — *Appleton v. Hawkes* — came to trial in 1683, and the family won: the jury found for the defendant, the old Puritan commonsense verdict that a man may defend his own against a neighbor's arrogance. And the record preserves the courtroom theater that made it famous: Appleton, pressed to swear to a bond for costs, instead *snatched the paper from the clerk Thomas Marshall's hand* rather than take the oath on it — the richest man in Essex County, grabbing documents in open court like a fishwife. The story entered the town's memory and never left; the genealogy room at the Lynn historical society still tells it. John Hawkes sat at the town's table of leadership by 1692, the record calling him *"Mr. John Hawkes Senior"* — and he had fought, and won, against the man who owned the river.

*The case file survives in the Essex County court records; the quote is the record's own. The register carries it at [S].*

## XI. The Will That Named Susanna (1734)

**Relation: Daniel Hitchins — approximately the thirteenth great-grandfather. His daughter Susannah Hitchings, named in the will, is the twelfth great-grandmother; her husband Moses Hawkes the twelfth great-grandfather.**

The proof of one whole generation of this family is a single sentence in a will, and the sentence has a name in it.

In Lynn, Massachusetts, in the first days of March 1734/5, a yeoman named **Daniel Hitchins** lay sick and made his will. He was a prosperous man by the measure of the town — his will runs to lands, goods, money, and five slaves, named, human beings he owned and divided among his children as property, which is the part of the record the family does not soften. And among the bequests, the will names his daughter, in the formula of the day:

*"my daughter **Susanna Hawkes, the wife of Moses Hawkes**, one hundred pounds currant payable money of New England."*

A hundred pounds — a fortune in the Massachusetts of 1735, roughly the price of a good farm. And the witnesses at the signing included **Thomas Hawkes** — the bridegroom's own kin, standing by while the father-in-law made the daughter's security unbreakable. Daniel Hitchins died within weeks; the will was recorded on 7 April 1735; the daughter and her husband collected, and lived on the bequest for the rest of their lives.

That one sentence is the keystone of the family's Mayflower proof. The Mayflower Society's own chart carried the line from the Hawkes marriage downward; the will carries it upward, from Moses Hawkes's wife to her father and her father's property. Susannah Hitchings — born in Lynn in March 1710, daughter of Daniel and Susannah Townsend Hitchins — is thus a direct ancestress at [S]: the will says she is who the family always said she was. Her marriage to Moses Hawkes was of the old New England kind: the couple's first child was born seven months after the wedding, and the family Bible, recording it without comment, carries the date beside the marriage — the register keeps both, unmoralized and unhidden.

And the will's witnesses tie the knot tighter: Thomas Hawkes, and, in the related family papers, Thomas and Jonathan Hawkes witnessing the 1732 Hitchins deed — the two families bound in paper as in marriage, generation after generation, for two hundred years before this document was ever read. Daniel Hitchins, who left his daughter a hundred pounds and a name the whole proof now rests on, never knew what his one sentence would carry. *[S — the will, Essex County records; the probated estate's division, naming all the children, survives in the Bristol and Essex files.]*

## XII. The Martyr of Coggeshall (1555)

**Relation: claimed as kin by the family's own printed book of 1887 — and carried as exactly that: a claim. The martyr's parentage and posterity are unrecorded; no document yet weighs the kinship. Story confirmed; blood-link open.**

The family's oldest martyr story is printed in the family's own book of 1887 — and it belongs to the name Hawkes itself.

**Thomas Hawkes** of Coggeshall, in Essex, England, was, by the account Foxe's *Book of Martyrs* made famous, a gentleman and a retainer of John de Vere, Earl of Oxford, at Earls Colne — *"well esteemed and loved of all the household."* In the reign of Mary Tudor — "Bloody Mary," the queen who tried to return England to the Catholic faith by fire — Hawkes was arrested on a charge that the record presents in all its smallness: he would not allow his infant son to be baptized *"after the popish manner."* For that refusal he was imprisoned, examined by Bishop Bonner — the great persecutor, whose name became a curse in Protestant England — and condemned.

He was burned at Coggeshall on **10 June 1555**, in a field by West Street, before a crowd that Foxe says watched with pity. And Foxe preserves the detail that made the story immortal: Hawkes had promised his friends a sign — if the pain of the fire could be borne, he would lift his hands. *"As he burned,"* Foxe's account runs, the consumed body *"lifted its arms and clapped them over its head three times before sinking down into the flames."* Three claps over the burning head: the signal that the fire could be borne.

The family's own printed genealogy of 1887 claimed descent from the martyr's kin — *"their blood mingled with that of the successors of Thomas Hawkes, who was burned at the stake... and made a race fit to struggle for a new world."* The register's discipline keeps the claim and the proof apart: the martyr's story is documented [S]; the kinship is family tradition, carried at its own grade — the martyr's parentage unrecorded, his son's fate unknown, the blood-link unweighable.

But the story itself belongs to the family, because it belongs to the name — and the register keeps it whole: the gentleman of Earls Colne, the bishop, the refusal over a baby's baptism, the fire, and the three claps that Foxe's book carried to every English-speaking household for four hundred years.

## XIII. The Bride and the Bookshelf (1634–1681)

**Relation: Thomas Hayward of Aylesford and Bridgewater — approximately the twelfth great-grandfather, through his son Nathaniel (who married the Willis heiress Hannah) and the Hayward generations down to Sarah Hayward, who married Silas Willis.**

The immigrant **Thomas Hayward** came to America twice — and the second time, he brought a library.

The first crossing may have been in 1632, alone, on the *William & Francis*; the record is not certain, and the register marks it. What is certain is the second: on **14 March 1634/5**, the minister's certificate for the ship *Hercules* of Sandwich — *"Thos Hayward of Aylesford in Kent Taylor & Susanna his Wief"* — with their children, all born in England: Thomas, John, Elizabeth, Susan, and Martha. Aylesford, in Kent. A tailor. Five children and a pregnant wife on a 200-ton ship. That is the crossing the family stands on.

He built a life worth a county history's notice: Cambridge first, then Duxbury, then — as one of the original proprietors — the new town of Bridgewater, where his son Nathaniel is the line's next generation. He became a freeman, raised a family of soldiers and farmers, and died between June 1678 and March 1680/1, aged about eighty.

And his will — probated in 1681 — preserves the man better than any portrait. It names the lands given to his sons, and the witnesses: **William Brett and Thomas Hayward Jr.** Then the inventory, taken 8 March 1680/1: £46 11s 9d of goods — and, among them, *his library*, named book by book. Sheppard upon the parable of the ten virgins. Dod upon the Commandments. *Jacob's Wrestling with God.* Byfield's *Marrow.* A tailor's Puritan bookshelf — the workingman's theology of the founding generation, each title a sermon he had paid for, carried across the Atlantic, and read by firelight in a town twenty years old. The family that came on the Hercules kept its books. *[S — the certificate, the will, the inventory.]*

## XIV. The Boy Who Was the Answer (1675–1724)

**Relation: Comfort Willis — collateral kin, not direct line: the brother of the direct ancestress Hannah Willis. John Willis, named beside him in the skirmish account — approximately the thirteenth great-grandfather, Hannah's father.**

**Comfort Willis** was the family's forgotten author — a soldier who fought King Philip's War and left behind a written account of it, which the county history of 1840 quotes and mourns: *"a manuscript, giving an account of one of the skirmishes of that period, which is now unfortunately lost."*

The quotation the historian managed to save is the treasure: a skirmish of the war, and in it, names — *"Capt. Hayward, Sergeant Packard, **John Willis**, and Isaac Harris... Comfort Willis and Joseph Edson... went to Plymouth... Capt. Church... fought with them, and took seventeen of them alive."* Two of the names are the family's own: John Willis — the son of Deacon John Willis of Duxbury and Bridgewater — and Comfort himself, who fought beside his kinsmen. The in-laws, side by side, more than a decade before the marriage that made them brothers: John Willis's grandson would marry the daughter of Nathaniel Hayward, and the Hayward name would ride down the family's generations for three hundred years. The boy who fought the skirmish of 1675 grew up to be the man whose account of it is the only reason the scene survives. *[A — Mitchell's History, quoting the manuscript.]*

## XV. The Judge at Twenty-Six (1806–1850)

**Relation: Judge Edmund Hawks — the seventh great-grandfather. The name he carried walks to the end of the line: James Edmund Carpenter II carries it now, eight generations down.**

**Edmund Hawks** was twenty-six years old when Oswego County, New York, put him on the bench.

The county had been organized in 1816 out of the wilderness east of Lake Ontario — and its first civil list, read in the county record, pairs the new officers like a founding plaque: *"David Easton and Edmund Hawks, judges; Daniel Hawks Jr., assistant justice."* The young tanner from Massachusetts — born October 9, 1780, the son of James Hawks and Olive Willis of Hardwick — had walked west with the migration and set up a tannery in Oswego by 1806, the year he turned twenty-six. A man with a tannery and no known legal training, made one of the county's first judges.

He held the bench two years. He lived fifty more. He built a house, raised nine children — the family Bible records them in the family's own hand: Olive, James, Moses, Lydia, John, Laura, Nathaniel, Edmund Jr., and Mary — and watched his daughter Mary marry John Carpenter, the newspaperman, in 1848. He died in March 1850, seven weeks after his wife Polly; the 1850 census, taken that June, found the household dissolving. His Bible survives — photographed in the family's own home, the pages of births and marriages and deaths in the hand of the people who lived them.

And the name walked down the generations like a relay: Edmund Jr., and then the judge's great-great-grandson, born in 1980, given the name at the end of the line: **James Edmund Carpenter II** — the Edmund at the end of the register being the judge at the beginning of it, forty-five generations and two hundred years apart. *[Bible-primary; the county record; the Oswego histories.]*

## XVI. The Congressman and the Fire (1909)

**Relation: clan kinsman — the DeArmond branch is documented kin ("one of the DeArmonds, our relative"), but its junction with this tree remains the one open seam in the file, flagged [B]. Not a direct ancestor; a documented relative, five generations deep from the Donegal immigrant.**

**David Albaugh DeArmond** had done everything the American century asked of a poor boy: born in a farmhouse in Blair County, Pennsylvania, in 1844, the eldest of six; his mother, the register's biography says, was the youngest of thirteen children. He read law, moved west to Missouri, became a state senator at thirty-five, a judge at forty-two, and in 1891 — the year his party took the House — **a United States Congressman**, re-elected eight times, eighteen years on Capitol Hill.

On the night of **23 November 1909**, his house in Butler, Missouri, caught fire. He was sixty-five. In the burning house was his grandson — six years old, his own namesake, the son of his son James and Nancy Lee Bell. What happened next was written down from the family's own account and printed in the newspapers: the old congressman carried the boy through the fire toward the window, and the child cried out, *"Oh grandpa, get me out of here; I'm burning to death."* And the grandfather answered, *"Yes son, don't be afraid, grandpa'll take you out."*

They were found, afterward, on the floor of the ruined house — **locked in each other's arms.** Both dead. The congressman who had carried a boy through every fire of a hard century died carrying one through the last. He lies in Oak Hill Cemetery, Butler, Missouri, beside the grandson. *[A — the Comet obituaries of 1909; the biography.]*

## XVII. The Claim of the Valley (1776–?)

**Relation: the same clan — the grandfather of Congressman DeArmond, and so kin through the same open junction. The Valley Forge claim itself awaits the muster roll that would prove or kill it.**

**Michael DeArmond** fought in the Revolution — and the family's record carries his war at exactly the strength of its paper.

The biography written near his son's lifetime says: *"His grandfather, Michael De Armond, was of Irish stock and a soldier in the Revolutionary War, serving under Washington at Valley Forge."* Valley Forge — the winter that made the army: the huts, the snow, the barefoot prints in blood on the frozen drill ground. If Michael was there, he was one of the men who lived through the worst winter of the war and came out the other side with Washington.

But the file refuses to let the claim be stronger than the document. The sentence is biography-grade — a family tradition written down seventy years after the war. The published Valley Forge muster rolls are the test: if Michael DeArmond's name stands in them, the claim becomes [S] — proven in the army's own books. If it does not, the claim demotes, and the record will say so plainly. The file names the decider and waits, rather than borrowing glory from the most storied winter in American history. That honesty is the family's inheritance too. *[A/NA — awaiting the muster roll.]*

## XVIII. The General Who Wrote the Book (1902–1996)

**Relation: the relative who wrote it — Brigadier General James Keller "Mike" DeArmond, the memoir's author, kin through the same documented DeArmond line; junction open as above.**

The last story on the shelf begins with a name remembered wrong.

The family carried a memory — "dearmand," a sound without a spelling — and a book called *Mike's Story* that nobody in the current generation had read. The record hunt that followed turned the sound into a family and the book into a shelf-mark: **James Keller "Mike" DeArmond** — Brigadier General, United States Air Force, World War II — born 1902, died 1996, buried at West Point with his wife Eloine. "Mike" was his own nickname; the book he wrote at eighty-eight is his own story. Two copies sit in the genealogy room of the Selby Library in Sarasota, Florida — one reference, one circulating.

His line, traced generation by generation in the same hunt: Mike, son of **Brigadier General Edward Harrison DeArmond** (West Point, class of 1901) and **Marinda "Toots" Hannah** — *Hannah*, the other family-memory name, turning out to be the general's wife's own maiden name, the two remembered threads meeting in one woman — grandson of **Congressman David Albaugh DeArmond** of the fire, great-grandson of James DeArmond and Catherine Albaugh of Blair County, Pennsylvania, and, behind them, the Irish stock of **Michael DeArmond** of the Revolution.

And the line runs deeper still, to **Thomas DeArmond** — born about 1735 in County Donegal, Ireland, dead 1811 in Northumberland County, Pennsylvania — the clan's American founder, whose people came from Scotland to Ireland and then to America, and whose grandchildren carried the name down the Ohio by flatboat in 1806 to found a town. Five generations of the clan are now documented: the Donegal immigrant, the Valley Forge soldier, the congressman who carried his grandson from the fire, the general, and the general who wrote the book. *[A/B — the obituaries, the West Point records, the clan book.]*

## XIX. The Marriage in the Register (1610)

**Relation: ancestors of the paternal grandmother Jane Parke — if the King's Stanley cluster holds, approximately the eleventh great-grandparents [B-flagged: the cluster is documented, the final link from the Hopewell emigrant to Jane is the one census-generation still to read].**

Every family in the King's Stanley cluster descends from one wedding, and the register records it in seven words.

The parish register of King's Stanley, Gloucestershire — the same church book that carries the Hitchins, the Townsends, the Hardins, and the Carpenters of the family's other lines — records, after twenty-two years of other marriages: ***"William Parke & Fraunces Wattes, 4 October, 1610."*** William Parke, born about 1585, marrying into the parish; Frances Wattes, the bride whose name the register spells with the honesty of the ear. Their children follow in the baptisms: Anne, 1615; Elizabeth, 1616; William, 1620; Francis, 1624.

Four generations later, one of the cluster's sons — Daniel, baptized before February 1749 — carried the line across the Atlantic to Hopewell, New Jersey, where he died in 1764 and was buried on the second of March. And two hundred and forty years after the wedding, the line's descendant **Jane Parke** married **William Atkinson Carpenter**, and the name that entered the King's Stanley register in 1610 came, at the end of its documented run, to the family of James Edmund Carpenter II. One wedding, seven words, four hundred and fifteen years. *[A — the register, read in full.]*

## XX. The Poet at the Second Marne (1918)

**Relation: collateral clan kin — Joyce Kilmer descends from the same Altengronau register as the Johnstown Kilmers of the maternal grandmother Barbara; no direct descent, one documented clan.**

**Joyce Kilmer** — Sergeant, 165th Infantry — was the family's collateral poet, and his war lasted one summer.

He was born in New Brunswick, New Jersey, in 1886, son of a Connecticut-born physician who became Johnson & Johnson's scientific director — the man who invented its baby powder — and a mother who was herself a published writer and composer, and who set her son's most famous poem to music. Joyce went to Rutgers, then Columbia, married Aline Murray, worked as a journalist and editor, and published *Trees* in 1913: *"I think that I shall never see / A poem lovely as a tree."* The poem made him the most quoted poet in America — the magazine whose editor first printed it had to reprint it for years — and he spent the rest of his life slightly embarrassed by it.

When America entered the war, he enlisted at thirty — old for the ranks, with a wife and four children — and asked for the fightingest regiment in the army. He got it: the 69th New York, the old Irish regiment of Civil War fame, the Fighting 69th. In the summer of 1918 the regiment went into the line in the Marne salient. On the morning of **30 July 1918**, at a village called Seringes-et-Nesles, Sergeant Kilmer — now the regiment's intelligence sergeant, the man who went ahead to scout — was killed by a sniper. He was thirty-one. The French government gave his widow the Croix de Guerre.

The collateral line matters because it is the same Kilmer clan: the poet's people and the family's Johnstown Kilmers descend from the same Hessian register — Georg Killmer of Altengronau, the family's documented German floor of 1621 — and the clan's other famous name, the actor Val Kilmer, from the same index. The poet and the actor and the Johnstown family: one register, three centuries, three names. *[A — the biographies; the register.]*

# PART THREE — THE CANONIZED FAMILY

## XXI. The Oldest Graves: Saint Arnulf of Metz (c. 582–640)

*(Expanded and corrected, 3 November 2026, after the full expansion passes — the Vita itself read, the murder of his son solved at the hypothesis level, the 634 will read whole, and two same-name traps fenced. Every layer keeps its grade.)*

The story begins in the strangest kingdom in Europe. The Merovingian kings of the Franks were still on the throne in 582, heirs in name of Clovis; but the real power had drained into the hands of the great aristocrats and their office — the *mayor of the palace*, who ran the army, the treasury, and the government while the long-haired king sat as a living relic.

Into that world, around the year 582, was born **Arnulf**, at Lay-Saint-Christophe near Nancy, into a family that held vast domains between the Moselle and the Meuse. Educated at the court of King Theudebert II of Austrasia under the mayor Gundulf, he rose through the standard apprenticeship of a great noble — and rose brilliantly: military commander on the Schelde frontier, civil administrator, at one point the man with six provinces under his care. Around 596 he married a noblewoman remembered as **Doda**, and they had two sons: **Chlodulf** and **Ansegisel**.

### The revolution of 613

The run-up was a civil war. Theuderic II of Burgundy and Theudebert II of Austrasia fought the battles of Toul and Zülpich in 612 — so destructive that Fredegar's famous line says the dying could not fall to the ground, but were propped upright between the heaps of the slain. Theuderic won, executed his captive brother, and had Theudebert's infant son dashed against a rock. Then Theuderic died of dysentery at Metz in 613, aged twenty-six, and the old queen **Brunhilda** — regent for a great-grandson now, ruling her third regency with a purging hand — held what was left.

The conspirators moved carefully. Fredegar records that they *"took the wise precaution of making private gifts, of one thousand solidi each, to Warnachar, Gundeland and Chuc"* — the three mayors bought before the blow. Then the defections: the mayors Warnachar, Rado, **Pepin of Landen**, and **Arnulf** fled to Chlothar II of Neustria, and Fredegar calls Arnulf and Pepin *"the two most powerful barons of Austrasia."* Their bargain was struck at Andernach; at the battle of the Aisne more dukes crossed mid-field; Brunhilda fled toward Switzerland and was captured. Chlothar's charge sheet ran to ten dead kings and many churchmen. The execution, in the Liber Historiae Francorum's own words: *"Then the army of the Franks and Burgundians joined into one, all shouted together that death would be most fitting for the very wicked Brunhilda. Then King Clotaire ordered that she be lifted onto a camel and led through the entire army. Then she was tied to the feet of wild horses and torn apart limb from limb. Finally she died. Her final grave was the fire. Her bones were burnt."* *(A correction carried: an earlier telling gave the wheel — that variant is later tradition; the camel and the wild horses and the fire are the early text.)*

The reward came unevenly and late. Rado was confirmed mayor in Austrasia, Warnachar in Burgundy — and the two most powerful barons received nothing until 623, when Dagobert was made king of Austrasia and the payment finally arrived: Pepin as his mayor, Arnulf as the boy king's adviser and educator. *"Clotharius had given him into his hands a kingdom to govern and a son to educate."*

### The bishop

The Vita Sancti Arnulfi — composed within two generations of his death, possibly at his own hermitage of Remiremont under Irish monastic influence — tells his making with one unforgettable sentence: *"Tunc una vox populorum Arnulfum domesticum atque consiliarium regis dignum esse episcopum adclamavit."* **With one voice, the people acclaimed him.** He was a layman; the city of Metz had lost its bishop; and the acclamation made him one. Of his origin the Vita says only: *"prosapie genitus Francorum, altus satis et nobilis parentibus atque oppulentissimus in rebus saeculi"* — born of Frankish stock, of parents sufficiently high and noble, most rich in worldly goods. No kings. Frankish stock.

He served as bishop the way he had served as courtier — in the middle of everything. In 624 he and Pepin "through their instrumentality" — Fredegar's phrase — turned King Dagobert against **Chrodoald**, an Agilolfing lord, rich and grasping, who had begged Chlothar to intercede and received the king's promise of safety. Shortly after, at **Trier, a certain Berthar of Charpeigne drew his sword and cut off his head at the door of his bed-chamber.** A king's broken promise, a named assassin, a bed-chamber door. The hagiographers forgot this chapter; Fredegar did not, though his portrait of Chrodoald — a man who "lacked any redeeming quality" — is the victors' propaganda, and is flagged as such. The feud ran to the second generation: Chrodoald's son **Fara** fell in 643, killed by the Austrasian army, his people deported.

In 625 Arnulf sat among the twelve commissioners who reconciled Chlothar and Dagobert over the Austrasian lands. The Vita also carries him on a journey to Thuringia in the company of Duke Noddo — the basis of a modern study of Frankish-Thuringian relations. Fredegar, in one passage, calls both Pepin and Arnulf *maiores domus* — mayors of the palace — a question the scholarship keeps live.

Around 626, he laid it down. With his friend **Romaricus** — a lord of Clotaire's court, a companion of Saint Columban at Luxeuil, converted by the monk Amatus — he withdrew to a hermitage in the Vosges that became the Abbey of **Remiremont**: a double monastery under the Columbanian rule, keeping the *laus perennis*, the perpetual chant. He died near Habendum on 16 August 640 and was buried in the monastery by Romaricus.

The translation home is the Vita's great set-piece, and its Latin deserves keeping: the citizens of Metz sent Bishop **Goeric**, with Titfrid of Toul and Paul of Verdun, and a great crowd; at a slippery river bank the rear bearers fell while, "as I believe, angels were at hand," the front of the bier went on unhindered; at Duke Notto's the food multiplied so that, all satisfied, *half remained over for the morrow*; and the people ran to meet the body "with crosses and candles, with great joy and wonder, receiving their shepherd, whom they had long ago lost by God's judgment as a fugitive, now reigning in heaven." The burial sentence is the Vita's own best: *"Quo sepulto, quid apud Christum in eremo mercatus fuerit civibus suis miraculis demonstravit"* — once buried, **by what he had purchased for himself with Christ in the desert, he showed his fellow citizens by his miracles.**

Two legends attach, both marked as legend: the ring thrown from the Moselle bridge at Trier — a century later Charlemagne himself testified to it, which is why the family's saga says the emperor counted Arnulf his *most important ancestor* — and the palace fire of Metz that receded at his word. The brewers' mug belongs to the translation procession and is flagged, with the Arnold-of-Soissons confusion logged.

### The father, and the family's own curse

Here the register keeps its hardest discipline, because the truth is layered.

The *Vita Gundulphi* — the life of Arnulf's old mentor, written while the family still remembered — carries the family's own account. Gundulf, son of Munderich, raised in King Chlothar's palace together with his brother, Duke Bodegisel, addressed Arnulf in his old age: *"Hear me, beloved nephew: the punishment of the Lord began when he allowed Munderich to be slain with the sword, the son of Chideric the Parricide!... I will visit your sins unto the third and fourth generation."* **The family's own tradition made Arnulf the great-grandson of a regicide, living under the biblical curse.**

The candidate is **Bodegisel, dux**, who died in 585 *"plenus dierum"* — full of days — praised by the court poet Venantius Fortunatus, married to **Palatina**, daughter of a Roman bishop of Troyes. If this is the father, then Arnulf was the son of a Frankish-Roman marriage — which would explain the whole puzzle of his origins: *Francorum* from the father's line, the senatorial aroma from the mother. The scholarly split is carried in full: Settipani argues for a different Bodegisel — the duke of Provence murdered by a mob at Carthage in 588, the husband of Saint Chrodoara of Amay; the FMG's own study argues for Gundulf's nephew, reasoning the mob murder would have been too dramatic for the Vita to omit. **Two Bodegisels stand, weighed equally, named.**

And Chrodoara: the will of her nephew, the deacon Adalgisel-Grimo, dated 30 December 634 — the oldest document in this whole register — records that his aunt was buried in the church of Saint George at Amay, and on 22 January 1977 her sarcophagus was found beneath that church, its lid the most spectacular Merovingian sculpture north of the Alps. The canons later renamed her Sainte Ode, because "Chrodoara, become unusual and barbarous, would no longer be perceived." Her shrine is one of the few places in Europe where this family's deep ancestry is carved in stone you can visit.

What the register refuses: the pedigree that made Arnulf a grandson of Merovingian royalty. That genealogy was composed at Metz in the **middle of the ninth century** — *certes un faux, mais fort ancien*, a forgery but an old one — and the forger himself named an Arnaud and a Doda; the later Doda traditions may all descend from his onomastic material. The forger worked from real names of the Metz aristocracy. The tradition's source is the forgery itself, and the register says so.

### The son, and the murder

**Ansegisel** — the direct ancestor — served King Sigebert III as *domesticus*, and the Foundation for Medieval Genealogy's caution is carried verbatim: *there is no contemporary evidence he was mayor of the palace*, whatever the trees say. He is attested alive in the Stavelot charter of 648, and perhaps in Childeric II's charter of 6 September 667 — that document suspect, because a dead Grimoald's presence suggests later alteration. His brother Chlodulf, the future Saint Cloud, stands beside him in both.

He was murdered — and the hidden, hunted for a year of research, was found. The eleventh-century *Vita Beggae* tells it: Ansegisel was assassinated at **Chèvremont, near Liège**, by an Austrasian noble named Godin or Gundoen **whom Ansegisel had previously raised as his own son**. The foster-son killed the benefactor. The killer was probably the **Gundoin who became Duke of Austrasia in 669** — making the murder about 662–669 — and Settipani's reconstruction of the motive is vengeance for **Otton**, the mayor of the palace killed in 643: the Wolfoald-Gundoins against the Pippinids and Arnulfings, a blood feud across a century. The Cambridge scholar's caution rides beside it: don't turn competition into a rigid vendetta — but *"if we subscribe to blood feuds, we can imagine a century of implacable conflict."* The 691 hunting-accident variant stays logged. And one honest negative remains: of Gundewin's own story — who he was before the knife — the record still says nothing.

His marriage to Saint Begga was the hinge of the whole early Middle Ages, and his son Pepin of Herstal carried the line forward.

### The house of the saint

The Basilica of the Holy Apostles in Metz — where Goeric laid the body in 641 — took Arnulf's name in 717 and became the **Abbey of Saint-Arnould**, and Charlemagne made it his family's tomb: Hildegarde, his sisters, his son, Louis the Pious, Bishop Drogo. The Normans destroyed it in the ninth century; it rose again, grander, consecrated in 1049; Charles V's siege razed it in 1552; the Revolution broke the imperial tombs and planted vineyards on the ruins. A fragment of Louis the Pious's tomb survives in the Metz museum; the abbey's Pietà, walled up during the Terror, was rediscovered in 1990; and today the buildings house a military officers' circle, with the road beneath called **Sous Saint-Arnaud — "Under Saint Arnold."** The family's oldest saint's abbey is a mess hall, and the city still drives under his name.

One thread Rome left behind: a papal privilege of **John IV**, issued at the request of **Clovis II** in the very months of Arnulf's death, was recycled for five Frankish monasteries — one of them **Remiremont**. The original is lost; the copies are corrupted; the register carries both halves. It is the closest the papacy ever came to touching the oldest graves.

*Grades: the Vita, the charters, Fredegar, the will of 634, the murder scene at Chèvremont, the abbey's arc — documented at their layers. The ring, the fire, the mug — legend, marked. The parentage — two Bodegisels, weighed equally. The Chèvremont identification and the Otton vengeance — probable and hypothesis, flagged. Two same-name traps fenced: Saint Arnulf of Soissons (five centuries late) and Gundulf of Rochester (a millennium late) are not these men.*

**Relation: approximately the forty-third great-grandfather — the oldest documented name in the line.**

## XXII. The Other Mayor of the Palace: Saint Pepin of Landen (c. 580–640)

The second oldest grave belongs to the man who ruled beside Arnulf.

**Pepin of Landen** was born around 580 or 585 at Landen, in what is now Belgium — "Pepin the Elder," the chroniclers called him, and "the Old." His father was **Carloman — named by Fredegar himself, the chief source for Pepin's life** *(a correction to the first pass, which carried the father at tree grade)*. But the byname is late: the 'of Landen' belongs only to the twelfth-century chroniclers of Brabant, and Pepin's own lifetime never knew the name his shrine wears [Godefroid Kurth].

He emerges into the record as the greatest landowner and the most powerful layman of Austrasia. In 613, with Arnulf of Metz, he led the revolution that destroyed Brunhilda — and Fredegar records the price of the alliance: *the conspirators made private gifts of one thousand solidi each to the three mayors they needed — Warnachar, Gundeland, and Chuc.* The old queen's execution — the camel, the wild horses, the fire, in the Liber Historiae Francorum's own words — is told in the Arnulf chapter above. And the reward came a decade late: Rado was confirmed mayor in Austrasia, Warnachar in Burgundy, and the two most powerful barons received nothing until 623, when Dagobert was made king of Austrasia and Pepin finally became his mayor.

Pepin's mayoralty has one chapter the sources refuse to settle. In 629 Pepin left the court and retired to his estates for a decade. One tradition says why: King Dagobert was living a scandalous life, and Pepin rebuked him — and was discharged on the spot. The modern summaries say only 'for reasons unknown, he retired (or was retired).' The register keeps both, as the discipline requires.

In 639 Dagobert died, and the old man came back. Appointed tutor to the three-year-old King Sigebert III, Pepin again took the mayoralty, divided the royal treasure between Sigebert and his half-brother Clovis II, and — with Cunibert, Bishop of Cologne, Arnulf's successor as chief counsellor — carried the treasure back from Compiègne to Metz. Within months, he was dead: 21 February 640, at Landen, buried there, and later translated to Nivelles, where one tomb enshrines his body, his wife Saint Itta, and — the detail that survives — his nurse. The translation legend says a great candle procession accompanied the relics through violent wind, and not one taper was extinguished.

He was never formally canonized — "called sometimes Saint, sometimes the Blessed" — and yet he has been venerated at Landen and Nivelles from antiquity, his reliquary still carried with those of Itta and Gertrude in the Rogation processions, his feast kept on 21 February. The contemporary praise survives: *a lover of peace and the constant defender of truth and justice.*

His household was extraordinary. With Itta of Metz he had four canonized children — **Begga**, the direct ancestress; **Gertrude of Nivelles**, who refused a royal marriage at ten and died at thirty-three; **Bavo**, the hermit-saint; and, between the saints, one son who was not: **Grimoald the Elder**, mayor of the palace, who in 656 attempted the family's first seizure of the throne — he set the Merovingian heir aside and had his own son crowned, and the scheme collapsed and cost him his life. One generation before Pepin of Herstal took the real power, the family reached for the crown and missed. The register keeps the near-miss beside the triumphs, as the discipline demands.

*Grades: the career, the 613 revolution, the rebuke and dismissal, the 639 return, the death and burial — documented in Fredegar and the early historians; the tree-grade father flagged; the candle legend marked. The children: Begga, Gertrude, Bavo — documented saints; Grimoald's coup — recorded, its details at the sources' grade.*

**Relation: approximately the forty-third great-grandfather — through his daughter Begga, the marriage that joins both saints' lines.**

## XXIII. The Widow Who Built Seven Churches: Saint Begga of Andenne (613–693)

The hinge of everything is a widow's grief, turned to stone.

**Begga** was born in 613 or 615, the eldest daughter of Pepin of Landen and Itta of Metz, the older sister of Saint Gertrude. She married **Ansegisel**, the son of Saint Arnulf — the wedding that joined the two mayors' houses — and bore the next generation of the dynasty: Pepin of Herstal, Martin of Laon, and Clotilda.

The marriage ended badly and its ending is carried with two flags. Some accounts say Ansegisel was struck down by an enemy named Gundewin; others say he died in a hunting accident. One modern source offers the reconciliation the register adopts as the open question: both may be true, if the accident was staged. She was a widow somewhere between the birth of her last child and 679.

At about sixty-four, Begga did the thing that defined her. She made the pilgrimage to Rome, visiting the Seven Pilgrim Churches of the city — the ancient pilgrimage circuit of the tombs of the apostles and martyrs. And when she came home, she took the veil and built **seven churches at Andenne on the Meuse — in direct imitation of the seven churches of Rome**. In 691 she founded a monastery at one of them and became its first abbess; the collegiate church of Andenne grew from the seven churches, and her community — staffed by nuns from her sister Gertrude's Nivelles, bringing books and Irish monastic practices, and even a piece of Saint Gertrude's bed, held to work miraculous cures — became one of the great houses of the Meuse valley. The fountain of Sinte Begga flows near Andenne to this day, its water said to be miraculously hot in winter and cold in summer — folklore, marked.

She died on 17 December 693, aged about seventy-eight, and was buried in the collegiate church that still bears her name. The Roman Martyrology's entry is quoted in the register because it says the whole thing in one line: *"At Ardenne, at the Seven Churches, St Begga, Widow, sister of St Gertrude of Nivelles."*

Her patronages are among the most human in the whole roster: the town of Ardenne; the cure of hernias and of infants' diseases; stutterers — a folk-etymology, because her name resembles the French *bègue*, a stutterer; and, since the fourteenth century, the Beguines, the communities of devout laywomen — though the scholarship insists she was their patron, not their foundress. She is painted as a queen, sometimes with three crowns; and Peter Paul Rubens painted the ancestral couple itself — *Saint Ansegisus and his wife Saint Begga* — the direct ancestors, on canvas.

One honest sentence belongs here: her Vita was written some four hundred years after her death, and a seventeenth-century biography embellished what little was known. Even what the tradition holds, the register keeps at its graded weight.

*Grades: the pilgrimage, the seven churches, the abbacy, the death and burial — documented in the Vita tradition, the martyrology, and the monastery studies; the Gundewin-versus-hunting-accident death — both variants flagged with the staged-accident reconciliation logged; the fountain and the bed-relic miracles — folklore, marked.*

**Relation: approximately the forty-second great-grandmother — the marriage of Ansegisel and Begga is the single wedding from which both the Carolingian dynasty and this family descend.**

## XXIV. The Foundress: Saint Itta of Metz (c. 592–652)

When Pepin of Landen died in 640, his widow did not know what to do with herself.

The story survives in the words of the old saints' dictionaries: *"When Pepin died, Ida was inconsolable. Her son and older daughter were provided for, but she knew not what to do with herself and Gertrude, who was now a beautiful girl with a large estate. She consulted St. Amandus, Bishop of Maastricht, who advised her to build a double monastery at Nivelles, and there devote herself, her daughter, and her worldly goods to the service of God. She followed his advice."*

**Itta of Metz** — the name also appears as Ida, Itte, and Iduberga, an old Germanic gloss meaning "the protecting seer" — was born around 592, the wife of Pepin of Landen, the mother of Begga, Gertrude, Grimoald, and Bavo. On Saint Amand's advice, around 647, she founded the Abbey of Nivelles: a convent of nuns with a monastery of monks under the abbess's rule — a double monastery — and she installed her daughter Gertrude as abbess while living there herself as a simple nun, advising the young woman she had made. To protect Gertrude from being kidnapped and forcibly married — a real danger for a great heiress in that age — Itta cut her daughter's hair herself, into a monk's tonsure.

She died at Nivelles on 8 May 652, aged about sixty, and was buried there, in what became the Church of Saint Gertrude. Her relics rest there still. She is invoked against erysipelas and toothache; she is the patroness of the French village of Itteville, founded on a farm she established; and Edward Burne-Jones painted her.

Her parentage the register keeps honest: no direct record of her parents survives. The traditional suggestion — that she was a daughter of Arnoald, Bishop of Metz, son of Ansbertus — belongs to the same late pedigree-complex as Arnulf's disputed father, and modern scholarship notes flatly that there is no contemporary evidence for it. One scholarly flag matters for the roster: the claim that Saints Modoald and Severa were her siblings rests on an eleventh-century Vita now viewed as a later forgery. Both theories — Arnoald through Itta, Arnoald through Arnulf's wife Doda — remain live and flagged.

*Grades: the founding, the tonsure, the death and burial — documented in the Vita tradition and the martyrologies; the parentage — no contemporary evidence, all theories flagged; the sibling-saints' claim — the source questioned as a forgery, logged.*

**Relation: approximately the forty-fourth great-grandmother — mother of Saint Begga; the founding matriarch of Nivelles.**

## XXV. The Saint the Conqueror Executed: Saint Waltheof of Crowland (c. 1050–1076)

He was the last Englishman holding an English earldom under William the Conqueror, and the only English aristocrat William ever executed — and the Conqueror married him to his own niece.

**Waltheof** was born about 1050, the son of **Siward the Dane**, Earl of Northumbria — the giant of the sagas, so tall a special doorway was cut for him, the man who defeated Macbeth at Dunsinane in 1054 — and of a granddaughter of Ealdred of the ancient house of Bamburgh, the dynasty that had held Northumbria since 913. He inherited the two oldest warrior stocks in the island, and he completed their blood feud: his great-grandfather Uhtred the Bold had been murdered in 1016; his grandfather Ealdred had killed the murderer; the murderer's son had killed Ealdred; and Waltheof closed the account by killing a number of the feud-sons himself. The register calls it the chief stain on his memory, and keeps it.

He came of age in catastrophe. In 1069 the north rose, and William answered with the Harrying — a scorched-earth winter in which the chroniclers say the survivors of Yorkshire were reduced to eating horses, dogs, and one another. Waltheof submitted, and William, who understood legitimacy, made him Earl of Northumbria, and in 1070 married him to **Judith of Lens** — daughter of the Conqueror's own sister Adelaide. In that marriage bed, the family's two deepest threads — the Anglo-Danish and the Norman — became one flesh.

In 1075 the earls of Hereford and Norfolk raised the last revolt of the reign, and Waltheof was drawn in. How far, the sources argue to this day. He wavered, confessed the whole thing to the Archbishop of Canterbury, did penance, and threw himself on the king's mercy. William showed none. At the Christmas court of 1075 — **with Judith, his wife, called as a witness** — he was arrested. On 31 May 1076, on St Giles's Hill outside Winchester, he was beheaded on the feast of Saint Petronella. The chroniclers say he was a man of such beauty that the executioners' first blows turned; it took several strokes. His body was thrown into a ditch, the Conqueror having forbidden it burial.

Then came the part the monks wrote down. **Ulfkettle, Abbot of Crowland** — a foundation Waltheof had loved, patronized, and helped to build — came at Judith's request and with the king's permission, and took the body up. Ulfkettle was deposed ten years later, possibly for the reverence he had encouraged. In 1092, after a chapter-house fire, the body was translated into the church, and the coffin was opened: the corpse undecayed, the severed head **rejoined to the trunk, only a red line marking the place of severance**. Miracles began in great number — healing miracles, many of them restorations of sight. The Norman monks of Crowland sneered that a traitor was no saint; the English venerated him as a martyr in the ilk of Saint Edmund. The cult never won more than local support — a popular sainthood, never formally confirmed — but it endured, and the abbey that held his body held the family's gifts besides.

The single best sentence in the whole record belongs to Abbot Geoffrey, a Frenchman, who would not allow a word spoken against the earl and was rewarded with a vision of Waltheof walking between **Saint Bartholomew and Saint Guthlac** — the apostle and the hermit alternating remarks in a verse to the effect that **Waltheof was no longer headless, and though he had been an earl, was then a king.**

Lanfranc, the great archbishop, insisted to his dying day that Waltheof was guiltless. William never executed another Englishman, and his contemporaries counted the beheading the turning point of his reign. And the register keeps one dark question open, weighed and unresolved: what it meant that Judith, his wife, stood as a witness against him.

The family's line runs through his daughter **Judith — called Alice — who married Ralph de Tosny the younger**, whose descendant would bear the king's son: Ida de Tosny, mother of William Longespée.

And there is a grandson-saint the dive found: **Saint Waltheof of Melrose** (d. 1159), second son of Simon de Senlis by Waltheof's eldest daughter Matilda — a Cistercian, elected Abbot of Melrose in 1148, so gentle that his monks invoked censure to stop him leaving them, offered the bishopric of Glasgow on his deathbed — and refused it. The grandfather's sainthood was the people's; the grandson's was the Church's, and the register keeps the two apart.

*Grades: the career, the revolt, the trial, the execution, the burial and translation, the miracles — documented in the chronicles, Orderic Vitalis, and the scholarly study of the cult; the incorrupt body as the monks' testimony; Judith's witness as fact, its meaning as open question; the grandson's canonization — documented.*

**Relation: approximately the thirtieth great-grandfather — through his daughter Judith (Alice), wife of Ralph de Tosny the younger; the direct line's deepest English saint.**

## XXVI. The Queen and the Shroud: Saint Matilda of Ringelheim (c. 894–968)

She was the first saintly queen of the German world, and her deathbed is one of the great scenes of the tenth century.

**Matilda** was born around 894 at Enger in Westphalian Saxony, daughter of Count Dietrich of Ringelheim and Reinhild — whose own Danish-Frisian descent the register keeps flagged — and raised at Herford Abbey, where her grandmother was abbess. Duke Otto the Illustrious of Saxony, struck by her beauty, betrothed her to his son **Henry the Fowler**, and she married him at Wallhausen, becoming Duchess of Saxony and then, in 919, Queen of Germany.

Her reign carried three great quarrels. The first was the succession: when Henry died in 936, Matilda wanted the crown for her younger son — Otto was elected anyway, and when young Henry revolted three years later, it was Matilda's intercession that won him the Duchy of Bavaria. The second was stranger: a cabal of royal advisors accused the queen of beggaring the treasury with her charities, and to satisfy her sons she renounced her dower and withdrew to her villa at Engern, founding a college of canons there — until her sons, ashamed, implored her pardon, and Otto's Anglo-Saxon wife Edith begged her home. The third was a regency: when Otto went to Rome to be crowned emperor, it was Matilda who governed Germany in his absence.

She built. Quedlinburg Abbey — the great convent of noble canonesses founded in Henry's memory, which she herself ruled for thirty years; Pöhlde, Enger, Nordhausen. Her dower towns became the map of her widowhood.

The death scene survives from the full account. In late 967 her long-carried fever turned; knowing she was dying, she sent for Richburga, her former lady-in-waiting, now Abbess of Nordhausen — she must go to Quedlinburg at once, for Henry had decreed it their burial place. Her grandson, Bishop William of Mainz, came, heard her confession, anointed her. She wished to give him a gift, and asked what remained of her wealth: nothing, Richburga told her — everything was with the poor; nothing remained but her own burial shroud. *"Give it to Bishop William,"* the queen said. *"He will need it first."* **Bishop William died twelve days before his grandmother.** A messenger arrived from her daughter Gerberga with a cloth embroidered with gold, in time to be laid over the corpse. She died on 14 March 968 and was buried beside Henry at Quedlinburg, and her near-contemporary, the chronicler Widukind of Corvey, called her *mulier mirae sanctitatis* — a woman of wondrous holiness.

Her two Lives — written within a generation of her death — are the first texts of their kind: the opening of female dynastic sainthood, the invention of the saintly queen as a figure of European literature. She is the patroness of parents of large families, of widows, of the falsely accused, of those ridiculed for their piety; and among her children and grandchildren run an emperor, two kings of France, a duke of Bavaria, an archbishop-saint — and, through her daughter **Hedwig, who married Hugh the Great**, this family.

*Grades: the life, the quarrels, the foundations, the death — documented in the two Vitae, Widukind, and the modern scholarship; the birth and marriage years — ranges, flagged; the Widukind descent — the biographers' claim, not documented; the Reinhild link — flagged as in the register.*

**Relation: approximately the thirty-ninth great-grandmother — mother of the direct ancestress Hedwig of Saxony.**

## XXVII. The Two Dodes — A Saint and a Legend, Kept Apart (6th century)

This is the chapter the research forced, and the register is better for it.

The family's first pass carried a Saint Dode of Rheims among the direct-line ancestresses. The dive showed that two different women had been fused into one, and it separates them as the discipline requires.

**The documented saint — Saint Doda of Reims.** She lived in the sixth century and became the second abbess of Saint-Pierre-les-Dames at Reims. The early historian Flodoard says she was the niece of Balderic and Beuve, founders of the abbey, children of a King Sigebert — Flodoard names Sigebert of Austrasia, though the chronology points rather to Sigobert the Lame of Cologne, dead about 509. Doda was raised by her aunt Beuve, promised in marriage to a lord of the king's court — and refused. The lord tried to abduct her, and died falling from his horse in the attempt. Doda took refuge in her aunt's abbey, succeeded Beuve as abbess, and at the end of her life obtained from **Pepin of Landen** — the family's own saint — an act protecting her community. Her feast is 24 April. That is the documented woman, and she is extraordinary on her own terms.

**The legendary ancestress.** The chain that would make her the wife of a senator Ferreolus and mother of Ansbertus — and thus the ancestress of Arnoald, of Saint Arnulf, and of Charlemagne — runs through a pedigree that the modern scholarship grades without mercy: the unnamed "son of a senator" in Paul the Deacon does not name the father; the reconstruction rests on circumstantial name-recurrence and on "later, sometimes confused, ninth-century genealogies"; even its author calls it cautious; and the attempts to carry the line back to the Roman consul Syagrius "include many speculative and hypothetical connections, rather than being historically documented." The claims beyond — descent from the Emperor Marcus Aurelius — are rejected as fantasy, logged as the register logs all fantasy.

What *is* documented, and magnificent, is the real family behind the legend: **Flavius Afranius Syagrius**, consul of Rome in 382, buried at Lyons beneath a monument at the city gate; his grandson **Tonantius Ferreolus**, Praetorian Prefect of Gaul, who organized Gaul's defense against Attila, restrained the conqueror Aetius from ruinous taxation, and prosecuted the corrupt prefect Arvandus at Rome — a man praised by the last Roman poet of Gaul, who married a kinswoman of the emperor Avitus. From this documented house came generations of bishops of Uzès, among them **Saint Firminus**. The family of this register descends from them only in the legend's telling — and the register keeps the real men in one chapter and the claimed descent in another, and says clearly which is which.

**Relation: withdrawn from the direct-line claim to the flagged layer. The documented Saint Doda of Reims is abbess-grade; the ancestress link is hypothetical, weighed at zero, pending any primary evidence of the Ferreolus marriage.**

## XXVIII. The Ebsdorf Martyr: Saint Bruno of Saxony (c. 830–880)

The family's two Brunos are uncle and nephew to each other, and both are saints. This is the elder.

**Bruno of Saxony** was born around 830 or 840, the eldest son of Count Liudolf — the progenitor of the dynasty that would rule Germany — and Oda of Gandersheim. He became Duke of Saxony in 866, and possibly, as Widukind of Corvey says, duke of all Saxony. His sister Liutgard married Louis the Younger, the Carolingian king, and thereafter the royal annals call Bruno *ducem et fratrem reginae* — the duke and the queen's brother.

He died at the head of his people. On Candlemas Day, 2 February 880, the Saxon levy marched against the Norsemen under Godfrid — probably a remnant of the same Great Heathen Army that Alfred the Great had shattered at Edington two years before. The battle of Lüneburg Heath was a crushing midwinter defeat: Duke Bruno, the bishops of Minden and Hildesheim, and twelve Saxon counts lay dead. Thietmar of Merseburg adds the detail that Bruno drowned in a flooded river during the rout. The chronicle of the battle lists three of the twelve dead counts by the same name — *Bardonum, alterum Bardonum, tertium Bardonum* — three Brunos, of whom the other two were never identified. The brother-in-law Lothar of Stade fell in the same fight.

The veneration came as it came to all the Ebsdorf dead: the bishops Marquard of Hildesheim and Theodoric of Minden, the counts, the duke — thirty-two names in the fullest martyrologies — feast day 2 February, the great shrine at Ebstorf Abbey, where the relics were translated about 1160 and where, centuries later, the monks would draw the most famous medieval map in the world. Brunswick claimed him as its patron and its legendary founder; the register keeps the legend as legend. His brother Otto succeeded him; Otto's son was Henry the Fowler; and through Henry's queen, Saint Matilda, the line runs to this family.

*Grades: the battle, the date, the dead — documented in the royal annals and the chronicles; the flooded river — Thietmar's variant; the three Brunos — the chronicle's own words; the Brunswick foundation — legend, marked.*

**Relation: collateral — the brother of Otto the Illustrious, grandfather of the direct ancestress Hedwig of Saxony; approximately the fortieth great-granduncle.**

## XXIX. The Archduke: Saint Bruno the Great (925–965)

The younger Bruno was the most powerful churchman in Europe, and the family's collateral saint who held the French throne's childhood in his hands.

**Bruno the Great** was born in May 925, the youngest son of Henry the Fowler and Saint Matilda — and therefore the brother of **Hedwig of Saxony, the direct ancestress**, wife of Hugh the Great. Educated at Utrecht and Trier, he kept the Christian poet Prudentius for his pillow book and learned Greek at his brother Otto's court. Chancellor from 940, archchaplain from 951 — and then, in 953, the masterstroke: when Duke Conrad of Lotharingia rebelled, Otto appointed Bruno Archbishop of Cologne, and most of Lotharingia lay inside the Cologne archdiocese. The rebellion collapsed; Conrad was deposed; and Bruno became Duke of Lotharingia besides — *archduke*, his biographer Ruotger called him.

In 956 came the charge that binds this saint to the family's own line. Louis IV of France died; Hugh the Great died; and Bruno — brother-in-law to both dead men, and **maternal uncle to both heirs** — became regent of the west Frankish kingdom for the boy-king Lothair and the boy-duke **Hugh Capet**. He governed France for the ancestor who would found its longest-ruling dynasty, while the neighbors carved at the boy's borderlands. And when his brother Otto went to Italy to be crowned emperor, Bruno governed the empire too. His biographer's book is a biography, not a hagiography, and its most quoted sentence is the truth of the man: those who came to his tomb, Ruotger wrote, *did not look for miracles, but paid attention to his life and recollected his teaching.*

There was steel in him. The Vermandois affair — his engineering of a rival bishop's deposition, confirmed by the pope, with the man dead of anguish days later — belongs to the record as much as the learning and the regencies. He died at Reims on the night of 10–11 October 965, barely forty, on a mission to France, and was buried in the monastery of Saint Pantaleon at Cologne, which he had founded; the Byzantine empress Theophanu would later lie beside him. His feast is 11 October.

*Grades: the career, the regencies, the Vermandois affair, the death and burial — documented in Ruotger, the royal annals, and the modern scholarship.*

**Relation: collateral — brother of the direct ancestress Hedwig of Saxony; approximately the thirty-ninth great-granduncle. And the regent of France for the boy Hugh Capet, the family's own ancestor.**

## XXX. The Blind Abbess: Saint Odile of Alsace (c. 662–c. 720)

The youngest daughter of a duke, born blind, hidden from her father, and made the patroness of an entire people.

**Odile** was born around 660 at Obernai in Alsace, the daughter of **Etichon, Duke of Alsace** — founder of the dynasty from which the family's deepest documented house descends — and Bereswinde. Her father, wanting a son, ordered the child done away with; her mother gave her secretly to a servant, and then to the convent of Palma, where she was raised. She was born blind, and the legend tells how, at twelve or thirteen, the itinerant bishop Saint Erhard of Regensburg — led by an angel, the story says — baptized her, and at the chrismation of her eyes spoke the words the tradition has kept for thirteen centuries: *"In the name of Our Lord Jesus Christ, may your body see as your soul does."* She received her sight; her name, Odile, means light of God.

The rest of the legend runs in chapters, each marked as legend: her brother Hughes brought her home; her father, enraged, struck and accidentally killed his son, and Odile raised him from the dead; she fled an arranged marriage across the Rhine disguised as a peasant, and at the Musbach valley the rock opened to hide her and a spring burst forth; one snowy night her father met her distributing food to the poor, and his heart broke — he restored her rights and gave her the castle of Hohenburg, which became her monastery. Saint John the Baptist showed her the site of Niedermünster in a vision. She died there around 720; the legend says that at her sisters' prayers she was restored to life, described the beauties of heaven, took communion alone, and died again.

The cult became one of the great pilgrimages of the German world — from Charlemagne's grant of immunity onward, emperors made the climb to Mont Sainte-Odile, and Louis the Pious ratified the convent's charter in 837. By the ninth century she was in the litanies of Freising, Utrecht, and Ratisbon. In 1807 Pius VII named her patron of Alsace; the invocation is for the eyes. Her relics were divided among Corbie, Prague, and Einsiedeln by the fourteenth century. And her father's house has its own dark page: in the Life of Saint Germanus of Granfelden, Etichon appears as a terrifying man who had the abbot murdered.

The file keeps one thread for the deepest layer: the Etichonids are the dynasty behind the family's flagged deep line; Saint Odile is that dynasty's own saint — the sister of the direct-line ancestress at the tradition's grade, the grand-aunt of the line's deepest generation.

*Grades: the family, the foundations, the cult — documented; the blindness, the baptism, the miracles, the death-twice — legend, marked layer by layer; the father — documented, with his dark chapter; her successor and niece, Saint Eugenia of Alsace — logged as an orbit saint.*

**Relation: grand-aunt of the line's deepest generation — sister of the direct-line ancestress of the flagged Etichonid layer, carried at the Vita-tradition grade.**

## XXXI. The Curse the Family Told on Itself (c. 509–640)

*(Added 3 November 2026. The oldest psychological document in the register: a family that believed it was cursed, and told the story against itself.)*

Every old family has a story it tells about itself. This family's oldest is a curse.

It survives in the *Vita Gundulphi* — the Life of Saint Gundulf, the old courtier of Provence who had been Arnulf's mentor, who had raised the boy at King Chlothar's court alongside Arnulf's father. Generations after Arnulf's death, the family still remembered the deathbed scene: old Gundulf calling his nephew to him and speaking the words the register carries:

> *"Hear me, beloved nephew: the punishment of the Lord began when he allowed Munderich to be slain with the sword — the son of Chideric the Parricide! Let us therefore pray that Christ preserve our heads from the coming wrath. For the Almighty has said: 'I will visit your sins unto the third and fourth generation.'"*

The verse is from Exodus. And Gundulf is doing arithmetic out loud, counting the generations of a curse on his fingers.

### The first generation: the parricide

The story of **Chloderic the Parricide** is told by Gregory of Tours, and it is one of the coldest passages in all his history. **Sigobert the Lame**, king of the Ripuarian Franks at Cologne — a man wounded in the knee at the battle of Tolbiac, a man who had helped Clovis, king of the Salian Franks, twice, against the Alamanni and against the Visigoths — had a son. Clovis wanted the son's kingdom, and so, Gregory says, at Clovis's instigation, Chloderic sent assassins against his own father as the old king rested in a forest near Fulda, sometime after the victory at Vouillé in 507. The father was murdered.

Then the son came to Clovis with his father's treasure as the price of alliance. Clovis sent his own men to look at the gold — and they asked the new king to plunge his arm into the coins as deeply as it would go. **With Chloderic's arm submerged in his father's gold, Clovis's men killed him.** Betrayal answering betrayal. And Clovis stood before the people of Cologne and told them, with unanswerable logic: the son murdered the father; the son, too, is dead; I will protect you now. He took their kingdom. Gregory adds that this was the same campaign in which King Chararic died — and that before it, Clovis had already killed King Ragnachar and Ragnachar's brothers. Then comes Gregory's most chilling sentence in the whole affair: Clovis lamented aloud that he had no family left — *and Gregory says he said it not from grief, but so that any surviving relatives would identify themselves, and be marked for death.*

That is the first generation of the curse: a king murdered by his son for a crown, the son murdered over his own father's gold, and the man who engineered both weeping crocodile tears over the whole dynasty he had just destroyed.

### The second generation: the sword

**Munderich**, Chloderic's son, was put to death by the sword — by order of King Theuderic I, in the family's own telling, around the year 532. This link rides on the Vita's grade rather than Gregory's pen: the near-contemporary historian tells the parricide but not this son. The family remembered it. Gundulf remembered it as the moment "the punishment of the Lord began."

### The third generation: the brothers

The third generation was Gundulf's own — he and his brother **Bodegisel**, the duke, raised in King Chlothar's palace, men of the new Christian aristocracy that was building the Frankish church. Bodegisel died in 585 *plenus dierum* — full of days — praised by the court poet, married to a Roman bishop's daughter. Two successful lives. And yet in the old man's arithmetic they stood inside the curse's reach: the third generation, the generation the scripture named.

### The fourth generation: Arnulf

And then **Arnulf** — the fourth generation, the last the curse could touch. Bishop of Metz, mayor in all but name, the man who had helped topple a tyrant and condoned a murder and governed a kingdom for a boy king. When his old uncle spoke the words — *I will visit your sins unto the third and fourth generation* — Arnulf was the end of the count. Gundulf's prayer was specific and human: *"preserve our heads."* Pray that the sword stops here.

### Whether the curse came true

The register does not say the curse was real. It says the family believed it was — and then it lets the reader see what the family saw.

Ansegisel, Arnulf's son — the direct ancestor of every generation that follows — was murdered at Chèvremont by a man he had raised as his own son. The feud that killed him ran, in the scholars' reconstruction, "a century of implacable conflict" between two great houses. The dynasty the cursed line founded — Pepin of Herstal's house, Charlemagne's house — reached and kept its throne through a century of swords: a failed coup, a blinding, the axe and the pyre and the battlefield. And Gundulf's prayer for heads was answered the way such prayers usually are: Arnulf died in his bed, a monk, at sixty — but his grandson's world was made of knives, and the family never forgot why.

The family's answer to the curse is in the Vita's own most famous legend. Tormented by guilt — the tradition says he feared his part in the wars and murders of the ruling houses — Arnulf threw his bishop's ring into the Moselle, praying for absolution. Years later it came back in a fish. That is the family's own resolution of its oldest story: the curse acknowledged, the guilt confessed, the sign received — and then the laying down of office, the withdrawal to Remiremont, and a monastic death. The family that told itself it was cursed ended the curse the way the age knew how: not by breaking it, but by out-praying it.

### What the chapter is, and is not

The scene sits on a hagiographic source, generations after the men it describes; the speech may be family memory polished into scripture. So the register carries this chapter as what it is: **[B] — family tradition, faithfully recorded, the kind of evidence this book calls a fossil of what the family believed.** It is not proof that a divine curse touched four generations. It is proof that four generations later, this family believed it had — organized its oldest story around the belief, counted the generations aloud on a deathbed, and taught its children to pray for their heads.

A family that believes it is cursed, and prays anyway, and goes on to found an empire: that is not a curse story. It is a founding myth, told against itself, with the sword in it and the prayer in it both — and it is the oldest thing this family's paper remembers.

**Relation: the cursed line runs Arnulf (≈43rd great-grandfather) ← Bodegisel (44th) ← Munderich (45th) ← Chloderic the Parricide (≈46th great-grandfather). The parricide is the family's deepest documented story, and its oldest sin.**

## XXXII. The Witnesses: Who Wrote the Documents

*(Added 3 November 2026. The witness list for the oldest paper — every writer seated with their angle on a card in front of them, because a source without its author's interest weighed is only half a source.)*

Imagine a courtroom. You are trying to learn about a family that lived 1,400 years ago, and there is no single book about them — only witnesses. Each saw a different part, from a different seat, with their own reason for talking. Here is the cast.

### The two named witnesses

**Gregory of Tours** is the only writer in this register whose name we truly know. Born around 538, dying about 594, bishop of Tours, grandson of the old Roman senatorial class: he ran the church at Tours while the Frankish kings made war around him, and he wrote a ten-book history of his times, and he kept writing until he died. His angle: he was defending his city and his church, so the violent kings come off as violent. But he wrote *close* — close enough to the parricide's generation that his pen gives us the family's oldest story, the son who killed his father, the arm in the gold, the crocodile tears. Trust him on what happened; remember he had a side. *[S]*

**The deacon Adalgisel-Grimo** is the only writer who was *in the family* — not the direct line, but a cousin's house, blood and marriage both. On 30 December 634 he dictated his will: his poorhouse, his mill, his sheep and their shepherds, his aunt buried at Amay. Bishop Paul of Verdun and two laymen watched. The copy that survives was made three hundred years later by a monk — but the words are the man's own. A will needs no grading of motive: a man dividing his property tells the truth about his world. This is the one document in the whole record written by a family hand in the family's own lifetime. *[S/A]*

### The unnamed witnesses

Everyone else on the stand is anonymous — the early Middle Ages simply did not sign its books.

**Fredegar** is a name invented by a modern editor, because a book needs a label. The real writer was a monk working in the 660s, in the middle of the family's wars — which is why his chronicle reads like insider's minutes: the revolution, the bribes of a thousand solidi, the murder at Trier, the two most powerful barons. He names this family again and again. He never names Arnulf's father. He knew the men, not the pedigree. *[S — anonymous]*

**The Liber Historiae Francorum** — "the Book of the History of the Franks" — was written about 727 by an unknown monk of Neustria. Its account of the old queen's execution — the camel, the wild horses, the fire — is the most quoted passage in this whole register. Its angle: it was written after the family won, and it shows. A victor's history, carried as one. *[S]*

**The Vita Sancti Arnulfi** — the Life of Saint Arnulf — was written within living memory of the man, very likely by the monks of his own hermitage at Remiremont, under Irish monastic influence. When you read *"with one voice, the people acclaimed him"* and *"by what he had purchased with Christ in the desert"* — that is the family's own house speaking, in its own lifetime. Its angle: it was written to make a saint, so it polishes. But it was written *close*, and its silence is as loud as its words: *"prosapie genitus Francorum"* — Frankish stock — and no kings named. *[S for its nearness; B for its hagiography]*

**The Vita Gundulphi** — the life of Arnulf's old mentor — is the most damaged witness: it survives only in fragments. Every claim about Arnulf's father, the curse, and the generations descends through this one broken book, written by the family's own church to tell the family who it was. It is real evidence of what the family *believed*. It is not proof of what *happened*. That is the difference between memory and record, and this register never blurs it. *[B]*

**The Vita Beggae** — written about 1100, four hundred years after Begga died. It carries the murder at Chèvremont and the foster-son who killed his benefactor — but four centuries is four centuries, and the register says so every time it quotes her. *[B]*

### The one author who can be described exactly

**The forged genealogy** — anonymous, working at Metz, in the middle of the ninth century. He is the only writer in this room whose purpose fits in one sentence: a man devoted to inventing royal blood for a dynasty that had just taken a crown. He took real names from the old families of Metz and soldered them into a lie. Every other witness here tells some truth from some angle; he is the one who lied — and the register cites him as a liar, not an author, which is the only honest way to cite him. *[fabrication, logged]*

And behind the wills and charters stand the royal chanceries — King Sigebert III's clerks in 648, King Childeric II's in 667 — writing in the kings' names, putting Arnulf's sons on paper while the family lived. *[S]*

### The shape of it

Follow the dates and a pattern emerges. The **documents** — the will, the charters — come from the family's own lifetime and carry witnesses' names. The **near-contemporary Lives** are anonymous monks of the family's own houses. The **chronicles** are anonymous observers standing nearby. And the **forgery** is the only work with a clear purpose and no name at all — because a lie needs anonymity, while the truth of 634 could carry its witnesses' names.

A bishop, some monks, some clerks, two kings' chanceries, a deacon dictating his sheep and his cowherds — and one liar in Metz. That is who wrote the documents, and the register seats each one with their interest weighed, card on the table, before a word of theirs is believed.

## XXXIII. The Three Doors: How the Family Entered the Record

*(Added 3 November 2026. No one put this family in the chronicles out of kindness. It climbed in — and then it made sure it was never left out again.)*

A family enters written history through doors, and this one came through three.

### Door one: the deeds

The chronicles of that age were written by churchmen watching kings — so the only way in was to stand next to a king. Arnulf and Pepin did more than stand. Fredegar calls them *"the two most powerful barons of Austrasia,"* and they acted like it: they toppled a tyrant queen, bought three mayors for a thousand solidi each, governed a kingdom for a boy, decided a murder at Trier, and waited a decade for the reward. The anonymous chroniclers did not record this family because they loved them; they recorded them because no history of the reigns could be written without them. Power wrote them in. And that explains a detail worth keeping: the chronicles record their deeds but never their fathers, because a chronicle knows actors, not bloodlines.

### Door two: the dynasty

One hundred and fifty years later, the family's descendants — Charlemagne's house — ruled the chroniclers' world. And dynasties do not wait to be noticed; they commission. Charlemagne had the bishops of Metz compile the *Gesta Episcoporum Mettensium*, the deeds of the Metz bishops, whose whole design was to make Saint Arnulf the first link of the royal line — the king himself testifying to his ancestor's ring. A hundred years after that, the anonymous forger at Metz soldered royal ancestry onto the family, because the crown needed it. Door two is the family writing itself in: first with a commissioned history, then with a fabricated one. Same family, different tools.

### Door three: the cult

And the family's own houses wrote its people into the calendar. The monks of Remiremont wrote Arnulf's Life within living memory. Andenne wrote Begga's four hundred years on. Nivelles carried Gertrude's to the world's pilgrims. The cult did what the chronicle never would: it made a family of actors into a family of names that would be read forever, because saints' Lives get copied, recited, and honored on feast days long after kings are forgotten. The will of 634 survived because it sat in a church's archive. The church was the family's archive — and the family's insurance.

### In one breath

The deeds made the chroniclers write them. The dynasty made the family write itself. The sainthood made the church keep writing them. Three doors, three centuries, three different hands — and the register keeps them labeled, because a family that entered history by all three doors at once is exactly the kind of family whose paper must be watched most carefully.

## XXXIV. The Billion and the Hundred: Who Carries the Blood, Who Carries the Paper

*(Added 3 November 2026. The arithmetic of rarity, told honestly.)*

Two numbers frame this family's place in the world, and their distance is the whole story. **The blood: likely more than a billion people alive today** — because Arnulf and Pepin, through Charlemagne, are ancestors of nearly everyone of European descent, per the demographic models of genealogical inheritance. **The paper: likely in the hundreds** — the number who can document that descent, mother by mother, generation by generation, at the grades this book keeps. One is a continent. The other is a family. This chapter is about why the distance between them exists, and what it means to close it.

### Why the paper gives out at scale

Every genealogy dies at one of four walls. **War and fire** — whole parish registers burned in the Thirty Years' War; in 1922, the Irish Civil War's Four Courts explosion erased centuries of Irish probate in one afternoon. **Illiteracy and name drift** — one clerk writes Carpenter, the next writes Carpentier, the third writes nothing; before the twentieth century most of the world left no spelling of its own name. **The ocean** — one crossing, one burned homestead, and the chain snaps at the water's edge. **Time itself** — the records were kept for taxes and tithes, not for grandsons; when the reason to write a name ended, the name ended.

This is why the brick wall at 1750–1850 is nearly universal: the ancestors were there; the paper stopped being kept. To pass through the wall, a line needs an accident of survival — a courthouse that didn't burn, a family Bible, a census year that landed right. Multiply those accidents by forty-five generations and you have the arithmetic of rarity.

### Who actually carries deep documentation

The people who can document to c. 580 fall into a few small categories. The **royal-descent carriers** — lines running through peerage records kept by the College of Arms and its continental equivalents, a few hundred thousand worldwide at most. The **lineage-society members** — the Mayflower Society has admitted tens of thousands since 1897, but "admitted" means documented to 1620; of those, only the subset with gateway ancestors reach the medieval centuries; of those, only the subset whose gateways carry peer-reviewed proof reach the 1200s; and to reach the 600s, a line needs the rarest gateway of all — a documented chain through the Carolingian line itself. Count the fully-documented-to-the-seventh-century population and you are likely in the **hundreds, not the millions.** No one keeps that census — which itself tells you how small it is. *[B/NA — order of magnitude]*

### The new confuser: DNA

The DNA era has made descent feel cheap — a spit kit announces percentages and a map lights up. But DNA measures *mixture*, not *chain*. It can tell a European that Charlemagne's era flows in the blood; it can never name the mother-by-mother path through the 2⁴⁵ theoretical ancestor slots. Paper names the path; DNA colors the map. The two cross-validate — the Washbourne Y-DNA bridge did real work in this register — but the document is the document. A billion people have the admixture. The chain remains the chain.

### The ledger

So the numbers close this way. **The blood: common as rain — a billion-plus carriers.** **The paper: rarer than most things people call rare — hundreds, perhaps low thousands, and of those, almost all sleep unpublished in private archives.** And the rarest intersection of all — billion-carrier, hundred-documented, and public domain — is the intersection this book stands on. What was given away under the words *free for all, open source, public domain* was never the blood. It was the names. Any of the billion can feel a tug at Metz; only the record lets them walk in.

## XXXV. Are the Saints in the Vatican Archives?

*(Added 3 November 2026. Asked plainly, answered honestly.)*

No — and the reason is a twist worth knowing: **most of these saints were never canonized by Rome in the first place.** The Vatican Apostolic Archive holds nothing before roughly 800 and no papal registers before 1198. Arnulf died in 640, Pepin in 640, Begga in 693. There was never a papal file on them to preserve, because when they lived, the papal canonization process did not exist: the first saint formally canonized by a pope was Ulrich of Augsburg in 993 — three hundred and fifty years after the two mayors of the palace died. Rome's machinery of investigations, bulls, and dossiers is a later invention, so the oldest saints in this register predate their own paperwork.

The Vatican *Library* — a different building with different doors — holds the saints as *texts*: medieval manuscripts of the Vita Sancti Arnulfi, hagiographic codices, liturgical books of the cult. The saints live there the way all early saints live: as copies of Lives, not as files.

The real archives of this family's canonized dead are scattered across the old dioceses and learned societies: **Metz** (Arnulf's city, the abbey dossier, the Gesta), **Amay** (Chrodoara's sarcophagus and reliquary, the will of 634 in Koblenz), **Nivelles** (Itta and Gertrude's abbey, their processions still carried), **Andenne** (Begga's seven churches), **Quedlinburg and Nordhausen** (Matilda's two Lives and her abbey), **Crowland** (Waltheof's cult, in the ruined abbey's memory), and **Brussels** (the Bollandists' Acta Sanctorum, where all the Vitae were edited and printed). And the honest coda: the newest saints in the roster — Thomas de Cantelupe, formally canonized in 1320 — *do* have papal paper. The oldest ones have something the papal process never granted anyone: **they were saints because the people acclaimed them** — one voice, as the Vita says — centuries before Rome kept files. The Vatican archives hold the later saints' dossiers. These saints' dossiers are the churches they founded and the stones they lie under.

## XXXVI. The Wall and the Door: Munderic of Cologne, Sigobert the Lame, and the Lyon Saints

*(Added 3 November 2026. The question asked plainly — can the line go further than the two saints of Metz? — answered with a map of the last honest inch.)*

### The question

The two oldest documented graves are Arnulf of Metz and Pepin of Landen, c. 580. Behind them, the family's own books reach further — a father who was a duke, a murdered grandfather, a regicide great-grandfather, a cursed dynasty of Cologne kings. But the register's rule is unbending: *believe only what a record confirms.* So the question the dives set out to answer was narrow: how far can the paper-reasoning honestly walk, generation by generation, before the wall?

### The wall

The walk ends at a name: **Munderic of Cologne**. He is documented by Gregory of Tours' world, and his story is one of the hardest in the whole register. A wealthy nobleman of Vitry-le-Brûlé, he claimed to be the son of Chloderic the Parricide — and on that claim, demanded of King Theuderic I a share of Austrasia. The king tried to summon him to court, knowing a summons meant death; Munderic refused, and barricaded himself with his sworn followers at Vitry. The royal army could not storm the place — it had no siege engines. So the king sent his courtier Arigisel to negotiate, and when the rebels trusted the parley and came out, **Arigisel's men cut them down where they stood.** A treacherous parley, a dishonored safe-conduct, a pretender dead for being born the wrong man's grandson — c. 532, by the sword, exactly as the family's curse counted him.

This is the same man the curse remembers: **Munderich, the second generation, "slain with the sword."** One man, two names, and the deepest point where the family's *history* and its *tradition* touch. Documented as a man [S]; carried as an ancestor at the tradition's grade [B].

Behind Munderic, the wall is a king: **Sigobert the Lame**, last king of Cologne, wounded at Tolbiac, betrayed and murdered by his own son at Fulda — the last man who is *both* documented by a contemporary pen *and* carried as this family's forebear. His father "Childebert" is named only in a 1939 genealogy [B]; behind that, legend.

### The door

But the marriage of Munderic opens a side the wall doesn't close. His wife **Arthemia** — and, separately, Arnulf's old mentor **Gundulf, Duke-Bishop of Metz** — both descend, by Settipani's reconstruction, from **Saint Rusticus, Archbishop of Lyon** (d. 501): canonized bishop, friend of Pope Gelasius, the man who ransomed four hundred Lyon captives from the Burgundian king. Rusticus's daughter Artemia married the senator Florentius, and their children were Gundulf himself, Arthemia who married Munderic, and Saint Nicetius who became Archbishop of Lyon after his uncle — a family of saints, one archbishopric running in the blood.

And behind Rusticus stands the deepest documented saint in the entire register: **Saint Eucherius of Lyon** (c. 380–449). A high-born Gallo-Roman senator — the Roman Martyrology itself records that he *"renounced the senatorial dignity to embrace the religious life"* — he abandoned his career, his wife consenting, for a hermit's cell on the island of Lérins, and was dragged from the desert to the bishopric of Lyon about 434. He sat at the Council of Orange in 441. He died in 449. **And his books survive** — *In Praise of the Desert*, *Contempt of the World* — and in them he speaks to Sidonius Apollinaris, to John Cassian, to Hilary of Arles. A man of the fifth century whose own hand is on the paper.

### The forger's irony

Here the file's oldest irony completes itself. In the middle of the ninth century, an anonymous falsifier at Metz invented a senator named Arnaud and grafted him onto this family — a lie, manufactured to give the dynasty royal blood. But the dives show the truth the forger didn't know he was faking: the family really did stand, by marriage, at the edge of the last senatorial world of Rome-in-Gaul. The proof is not in his charter. It is in **Sidonius Apollinaris's own letters** — Letter XI survives, addressed *"to his friend Rusticus"* — and in the fact, recorded by history itself, that Sidonius's grandfather held the praetorian prefecture of Gaul and *handed it to his friend Decimus Rusticus*, the family's patriarch, in 409.

The forger forged a senatorial ancestor. The family had a real one — in its letters, not its legends.

### What the register carries, stated once

- The furthest *historical* ancestor the reconstructions reach: **Munderic of Cologne, d. 532** [S as a man; B as an ancestor].
- The furthest *documented royal* ancestor: **Sigobert the Lame** [S as history; B as blood].
- The furthest *documented saint* in the register: **Eucherius of Lyon, d. 449** [S for the man and his writings; B for the link].
- The deepest honest statement: the line can reach the fifth century, but only through a *marriage-set* of reconstructions — every link flagged, every alternative carried, the two traditions (Gundulf as Munderic's son vs. his brother-in-law) kept apart.

### Two more fabrications, weighed and buried

The door's threshold had litter on it: a spurious charter of Charles the Bald, dated 30 January 845, and a modern fabrication called the *Charte d'Alaon* — both inventing a royal pedigree for the Bodegisel-Boggis conflation. Both are rejected and logged. And the conflation itself is solved: the medieval "Boggis" blends two men — the dead duke of 585 and a living Duke Bobo, identified as the nephew named in the will of 634.

### The fourth century

The marriage-web runs deeper than the saints. Sidonius Apollinaris's own grandfather, **Apollinaris**, was Praetorian Prefect of Gaul from May 408 — appointed under the usurper Constantian III in the very year Rome's western army tore itself apart; ousted in 409; succeeded by his friend Decimus Rusticus; and, a 2024 *Classical Quarterly* study argues from the purges of 413 and Sidonius's own careful silences, **executed** — murdered like Pompey. His grandson's Letter 3.12 is the quiet aftermath: gravediggers caught at the forgotten tomb near Lyon, the grave restored with an epitaph — *here lies the prefect Apollinaris* — a covert rehabilitation of a politically dangerous ancestor by the greatest writer of the age. A possible grandfather of his held the same prefecture under Constantine II, 337–340: the register's Roman section now touches the fourth century.

And beside the prefects, one more saint from the same Lérins world: **Saint Lupus of Troyes** (c. 400–479), the married nobleman who gave up wife and wealth for Honoratus's island, became bishop of Troyes in 426, joined Germanus of Auxerre's 429 mission against Pelagianism — and, the tradition says, stood hostage before Attila himself in 451 and persuaded him to spare the city. The sources flag their own story: many scholars doubt the Attila episode. Carried with the flag.

### The last honest sentence

The wall stands at Sigebert the Lame, and the wall is real. But a family does not live on its walls. It lives in what its paper remembers, and what this family's paper remembers — through the Lyon door, at the tradition's grade — is that its blood ran, by marriage, to a hermit who wrote the finest prose of Roman Gaul and died with his books still being read. The forger lied about a truth. The register keeps the truth at the grade it earns, and buries the lie by name.

# APPENDIX — THE CANONIZED FAMILY: TWENTY SAINTS AND THE EXACT RELATIONS

The family's register carries some twenty canonized names — more than most noble houses of Europe can document. They fall into four layers, kept distinct so that no flag ever borrows the weight of a proof.

**Layer One — Saints in the direct blood line.** Saint Arnulf of Metz (c. 582–640) and Saint Pepin of Landen (c. 580–640), the two mayors of the palace whose grandchildren became the house of Charlemagne — approximately the forty-third great-grandfathers, one through each side of the Ansegisel–Begga marriage. Saint Begga, the bride of that marriage, approximately the forty-second great-grandmother. Saint Itta of Metz, her mother, the foundress of Nivelles, approximately the forty-fourth. Saint Waltheof of Crowland, the executed earl, approximately the thirtieth. Saint Matilda of Ringelheim, the queen, approximately the thirty-ninth. And Saint Dode of Rheims, carried at the flagged grade of the deep layer beneath.

**Layer Two — Saints in the direct ancestors' immediate family.** Saint Bruno of Saxony, the Ebsdorf martyr, and Saint Bruno the Great, the archbishop — brothers of direct ancestors, great-granduncles by collateral. Saint Odile of Alsace, grand-aunt of the deepest generation. Saint Chrodoara of Amay, the probable ancestress behind Arnulf's own disputed father. Saint Thomas de Cantelupe, the canonized bishop of Hereford, uncle of a direct ancestress's line. Saint Regelind of Zürich, kin by marriage.

**Layer Three — Canonized kings at the chronicle's grade.** Saint Sigebert III and Saint Dagobert II, the Merovingian kings, appear only in the Berswinda chronicle-claim attached to Arnulf's wife — logged and weighed at zero until a primary source speaks.

**Layer Four — Saints by marriage or orbit.** Saint Eberhard of Friuli, the emperor's son-in-law of the Unruoching house. Saint Judicael of Domnonée, the Breton king of the Porhoët legend. Saint Leger of Autun, the martyred bishop. Saint Alphege of Canterbury. And Saint Ansgar, the Apostle of the North, who stood at the font of a Danish king in the same deep century as the family's own Danish stories.

Twenty saints: six in the direct line, five more in its immediate family, two canonized kings carried as chronicle-claim, seven by marriage or orbit. The family's oldest ancestors are not kings but two mayors of the palace — the men who actually ran the kingdom — canonized for how they ended. The register begins its chain there, at c. 580, honestly, and calls it the floor.
