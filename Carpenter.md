## 0. How to Read This Document

**Evidence grades** used throughout:

- **[S]** — Primary or official source: court records, county histories published at the time, newspapers of record, Mayflower Society publications.
- **[PR]** — Peer-reviewed genealogy: *New England Historical and Genealogical Register* (NEHGR), *The American Genealogist* (TAG).
- **[A]** — Published genealogical book or archive-grade transcription (1901 Carpenter genealogy, 1915 Webster genealogy, Hough/Landmarks county histories, published vital records).
- **[B]** — Well-sourced user trees (WikiTree profiles with citations, curated family sites).
- **[FS]** — FamilySearch/MyHeritage user-contributed profiles. Useful as *leads*; every FS fact needs a primary record behind it before it counts as proof.
- **[H]** — Working hypothesis (clearly labeled).

**Three golden rules used in compiling this history:**
1. *Collect, don't adopt.* Family traditions are evidence of what the family believed, not proof of what happened. Each tradition is logged in Section 12 with its verification status.
2. *Names are fossils.* Middle names in America usually preserve a mother's maiden name or a revered ancestor: **Edmund, Moses, Dunton, Atkinson, Webster** in this family each mark a real person located in the record.
3. *One record can close a generation.* The open items in Section 11 are each one document away from resolution.

## A Short Glossary (for readers new to genealogy)

- **Vital records** — government- or church-kept registers of births, marriages, and deaths. Before the 1850s in America, these were kept by towns and churches, not states — which is why "the Petersham vital records" means one specific published book.
- **Silver Book** — one of the Mayflower Society's compiled volumes, giving every documented descendant of a given Mayflower passenger for the first five generations. If you can document yourself into any name in a Silver Book, the Society will help you complete the proof.
- **Mayflower Compact** — the self-government agreement signed aboard the *Mayflower* in November 1620 by the adult men of the party.
- **Yeoman / husbandman** — a working farmer who owned or rented his land; below a gentleman, above a laborer. Most of your ancestors were yeomen.
- **Proprietor of a newspaper** — the owner and effective editor of a paper. In 1830s America this made John Carpenter a public figure of his county.
- **Associate judge** — a county trial judge; Oswego County's first bench in 1816 had several, Edmund Hawks among them.
- **Monthly meeting (MM)** — the local unit of Quaker church government; its records are among the best colonial sources because Quakers recorded everything.
- **Indenture / apprenticeship record** — a bound labor contract; guild apprenticeship registers (like the Blacksmiths' Company record naming Isaac Allerton) often preserve parentage.
- **Collateral kin / affinal** — relatives by blood but not direct descent ("collateral"), or by marriage ("affinal," sometimes called "kin by marriage"). Several famous connections here are of these kinds, and the document says so every time.
- **Half-cousin** — cousins sharing only one ancestor in the relevant couple (here: two lines of Isaac Allerton's children by different wives).
- **æ** (on old gravestones) — Latin *aetatis*, "in the year of age"; "æ 58" means the person was 58 years old.
- **"Last survivor of the Mayflower"** — Mary Allerton Cushman, Isaac's daughter, outlived every other passenger, dying in 1699.
- **GEDCOM / tree platforms** — FamilySearch and MyHeritage are shared family trees built by thousands of users; treat any single fact there as a lead until a primary record confirms it.
- **"Collect, don't adopt"** — this document's rule for family tradition: write it down faithfully, tag it, and only *believe* it when a record independently confirms it.

## The Story in Plain English

*(If the sections below ever feel technical, live here. This is the whole document without the citations.)*

Somewhere around 1620, a well-off young Londoner named **Isaac Allerton** — a tailor's son from the Suffolk village of East Bergholt — broke his apprenticeship, fled to Holland, married, and sailed on the *Mayflower*. He outlived the first winter that killed half the company, remarried the daughter of the colony's religious leader, got rich, quarreled with the Puritans, and died in Connecticut in 1659. Two of his daughters married two brothers of a Marblehead shipwright's family, and their children married two children of a Lynn farmer named **John Hawkes** — which is how, if one 1780 birth entry reads the way every clue says it should, **Isaac Allerton became your eighth-great-grandfather.**

His grandchildren's grandchildren drifted west with the country — from Massachusetts farm towns to a new county on Lake Ontario, where in 1816 a young judge named **Edmund Hawks** helped organize Oswego County, New York. His daughter **Mary** married **John Carpenter**, who had come up from New Jersey and owned the local newspaper for fifteen years. Their son was named **James Dunton Carpenter** — Dunton being the family next door that had intermarried the Hawkses — and the names keep walking down the generations like stepping stones: James **Dunton** Carpenter Jr. married **Emily Atkinson**; their son **William Atkinson Carpenter** married a **Parke**; their son was **James Edmund Carpenter** — *Edmund* for the old judge — and his son, born in 1980, is you.

On your mother's side, the stepping stones are different but the pattern is the same. A girl named **Louise Webster** married a New York farmer named Sylvester Abbott in 1831, and because of her, four generations of Abbott men have carried **Webster** as a middle name — your grandfather **Richard Webster Abbott** is the living proof of it. Louise's family walks back through Litchfield, Connecticut, to **Governor John Webster**, who helped found Hartford in 1636 and governed Connecticut in 1656. His family can be traced in English parish registers for five generations before he was born, to about the year 1485, in one Leicestershire village. **Noah Webster** — the dictionary writer — belongs to the *same* governor's family, through a parallel branch: the family's old claim of being "direct to Noah" is really a claim of being direct to the *same ancestor Noah had*. You and the dictionary are cousins.

The family also remembers three ships besides the *Mayflower*: the ***Bevis*** (1638), which carried the first Carpenters; the ***Diligent*** (1638), which carried the Pecks and possibly a Hawke brother; and the ***Gale of Whitehaven*** (1774), which carried **Andrew Hannay** from Galloway, Scotland, to Albany County, New York — a man whose clan, the **Hannays of Sorbie Tower**, your family still names as its own. The place where that Scottish thread might enter the American family is marked too: a woman born on the English–Scottish border in 1668 whose middle name was **Hannah**.

None of this is fantasy, and none of it is finished. Step back and the wider family comes into view. Through Adam Hawkes alone — one Norfolk farmer who followed his neighbors to Massachusetts — the family counts two presidents (Adams father and son) and, astonishingly, **Diana, Princess of Wales**, among its cousins, all through Adam's daughter Susanna and the Cogswells of Ipswich. Through the Cushman and Delano marriages come Franklin Roosevelt and a signer of the Declaration. Through the carpenter's trade and the *Bevis* comes an astronaut. One of the New England Websters became the dictionary; his family's published lists of descendants include Emily Dickinson, Johnny Carson, and a president named Hayes. None of these are borrowed celebrities — each is a cousin of a cousin, reached by marriage and descent through the same few families who kept marrying each other on the same coast of New England for two hundred years.

Every claim above sits in one of three boxes: **proven** (the newspaper, the judge, the governor, the cousin-branch to Noah, the Lee marriage, the Adamses, the princess), **one record away** (Allerton, the *Bevis* link, the Sorbie link), or **tradition, faithfully recorded** (the martyr legends, the oldest English pedigrees). Section 12 lists every claim in its box. Section 11 lists the five specific pages that would move the remaining names into the proven box — one of which, the family Bible, is sitting in your parents' house.

## 4. The Carpenter Immigrant Question — *Bevis* (1638) vs. Providence (1637)

### 4.1 The Rehoboth line — the *Bevis*, 1638 [S/PR]
The ***Bevis* of Hampton** (150 tons, master Robert Batten) sailed from Southampton, May 1638, with 61 passengers. On the manifest [S — Hotten's Original Lists]:
- **William Carpenter, age 62, carpenter, "of Horwell"** (Wherwell, Hampshire) — b. ~1575/76
- **William Carpenter Jr., age 33, carpenter, of Wherwell** — b. ~1605

**The rival branch (logged, not adopted):** a **Deerfield/Charlemont, MA** Hawks family — descendants of **John Hawks of Windsor/Hadley (b. ~1613, d. 1662)**, the *other* Hawkes immigrant, whose name appears in Windsor records as **H-a-k-e-s** — spread through Deerfield, Hadley, Charlemont, Gill, and Petersham's neighbors. ### 5.3 Isaac Allerton — the immigrant [PR/S]
- Born about **1586–87, probably East Bergholt, Suffolk** — the breakthrough identification (Caleb Johnson, Sue Allan & Simon Neal, *"The English Origin and Parentage of Mayflower Passenger Isaac Allerton,"* **NEHGR 173 (2019): 197–205**): son of **Bartholomew Allerton, tailor** (of Ipswich/East Bergholt) **and Mary**. The parish registers of East Bergholt do not survive before 1653, and the family were religious dissentents — which is why the trail was cold for four centuries.
- A 1609 Blacksmiths' Company apprenticeship record ("Isack Allerton son of Bartholomew Allerton of Ipswich in County Suffolk, tailor," bound to James Glyn) shows him breaking his indenture and surfacing in **Leiden**, where he married **Mary Norris, 4 November 1611**.
- **Mayflower, 1620**; signer of the Compact; assistant governor; the wealthiest Pilgrim; seven Atlantic crossings. First wife Mary died February 1620/21; married **Fear Brewster** (daughter of Elder William Brewster) c. 1626 — she died by December 1634; third wife Joanna Swinnerton.
- Children: **Bartholomew** (returned to England; d. Bramfield, Suffolk, 1658/59 — his will survives, transcribed by Leslie Mahler); **Remember** (m. **Moses Maverick**); **Mary** (m. **Thomas Cushman** — d. 1699, the last surviving Mayflower passenger); **Sarah**; and by Fear, **Isaac Allerton Jr.**
- Business quarrels drove him from Plymouth; he built a trading empire from New Amsterdam (house on Broadway, wharf, warehouse) and **died at New Haven, Connecticut, February 1658/59 — the only member of the Mayflower company buried in Connecticut** (marker on the New Haven Green, 1941). [S — Mayflower Society profile; Winthrop journal episodes]
- Sister: **Sarah Allerton**, who married successively John Vincent, **Degory Priest** (Mayflower passenger), and Godbert Godbertson, and came on the *Anne* (1623) bringing Isaac's daughter Sarah. A probable brother: **John Allerton**, Mayflower passenger (d. 1621). A possible kinsman (unproven): **Ralph Allerton of Great Bentley, Essex, burned at the stake 1555** — a Marian martyr; the family tradition of a Reformation martyr attaches to the name, on both sides of the Atlantic, in *both* the Allerton and Hawke families. [PR/B; lore flagged]

### 5.4 The Mavericks [PR — NEHGR 69 (1915): 146–159 (Elizabeth French); NEHGR 115 (1961): 248–253 (Hunt); NEHGR 122 (1968): 282–283]
Three generations of Devon clergy, documented in the parish and diocesan records:
- **Robert Maverick, clerk, of Awliscombe** (d. 1573; his predecessor at the Awliscombe church was installed 1554).
- His son **Rev. Peter Maverick, *alias Bull***, ordained 1573/4, vicar of Awliscombe from 1580, m. 1577 **Dorothy Tucke** (daughter of tenants of the Mayor of Exeter); died a violent death c. 1616. The alias has never been explained — the best surmise is that Peter was a step-son who took his step-father's name.
- His son **Rev. John Maverick** (bapt. Awliscombe 28 Dec 1578 – d. Dorchester 3 Feb 1635/6): Exeter College, Oxford (B.A. 1599, M.A. 1603); ordained 1597; rector of Beaworthy 1615–1629; **sailed on the *Mary & John*, March 1630**, and co-founded the church at Dorchester with John Warham. Winthrop's eulogy: *"a man of very humble spirit, and faithful in furthering the work of the Lord, both in church and civil state."*
- **Moses Maverick** (bapt. **South Huish, 3 Nov 1611** – d. 1686), founder of Marblehead: m. (1) by 6 May 1635 **Remember Allerton** (daughter of Isaac Allerton); m. (2) 1656 Eunice (___) Roberts. Their daughter **Rebecca** married **John Hawkes of Lynn** (1658); after her early death John married her cousin **Sarah Cushman**; Moses's 1686 will names **"Moses Hawks, the only surviving child of my daughter Rebecca."** Moses's siblings: Samuel (the famous colonist, emigrated first), Elias, Aaron, Mary (m. Rev. James Parker), Abigail (m. John Manning). The Cushmans are themselves Mayflower gentry: Sarah's father-in-law **Robert Cushman** was the Mayflower party's business agent in London and crossed on the *Fortune* (1621); her husband Thomas had arrived in 1623 and lived to bury the last of the passengers.
- **The guardian-uncle:** John's uncle **Rev. Radford Maverick** (b. 1560; vicar of Ilsington) **raised the orphaned Mary Gye as his ward** — her grandfather Robert Gye paid him "great sums" for her upbringing — and performed her marriage to his own nephew John in 1600. Hunt's surmise (unproven, flagged): the elder Robert Maverick's unnamed wife may have been a **Prouse**, sister of Mary Prowse — which would make the Mavericks and Gyes cousins and explain the wardship, the name "Radford" (after Mary's great-uncle, the murdered judge), and the phrase "cousin-german" in the records.

### 5.5 Adam Hawkes of Lynn — the English head of the line [PR — Great Migration 3:253–257; NEHGR 178 (2024)]
- Baptized **26 January 1604/5 at Hingham, Norfolk**; first documented in New England at Charlestown, 1634/5 (the Winthrop Fleet 1630 tradition is **not supported by evidence** per the Great Migration project); settled Lynn (Saugus); d. 13 March 1671/2. His first wife was **Anne, widow of the unidentified Mr. Hutchinson** (the first husband's identity established by Robert Battle, *NEHGR* 178 (Fall 2024): 307–318); by her: son **John Hawkes** (1633–1694, the Allerton son-in-law) and daughter **Susanna Hawkes** (b. ~1633). Estate: £817 11s (£672 real estate) — a prosperous yeoman. He spent decades in litigation with the Saugus Iron Works over flooding of his lands (his son John finally arranged the dam's destruction in May 1682).
- His probate distributes the family farm with explicit reference to **"Moses Hauks, son of John Hauks by Rebeckah Hauks, daughter of Mr. Moses Mavericke"** — the Allerton connection in the founding generation's own hand.
- **⚠ Parentage corrections (PGM):** Adam was **not** the son of Mary Cowper (her Hingham marriage to John Hawkes occurred in 1612/3, years after Adam's birth), and there is **no known association** between Adam and John Hawks of Hadley or Matthew Hawkes of the *Diligent* — the "brothers" tradition is disowned by the Great Migration project.
- **Notable descendants of Adam & Anne through their daughter Susanna Hawkes & William Cogswell of Ipswich:** **President John Adams** and **President John Quincy Adams** (per Gary Boyd Roberts, *Ancestors of American Presidents*, 2009), and — through the same Cogswell line — **Diana, Princess of Wales** (per Richard K. Evans, *The Ancestry of Diana, Princess of Wales*, NEHGS 2007, p. 330). The user descends through Adam's *son* John — so the presidents and the princess are the user's **cousins through Adam Hawkes** (they via Susanna, the user via John). *Lead flagged:* Moses Hawke (the user's line) married **Margaret Cogswell** — possibly of the same Ipswich Cogswell family; unproven.

## 6. The Lee Connection — "We are related to Francis Lightfoot Lee"

**The documented marriage:** **Hancock Lee (1653–1709)**, fifth son of **Richard Lee I "the Emigrant"** of Virginia, married as his second wife **Sarah Allerton (c. 1670–1731)** — daughter of **Isaac Allerton Jr.** of Westmoreland County and **granddaughter of Mayflower passenger Isaac Allerton** (and, through Isaac Jr.'s mother Fear Brewster, of Elder William Brewster). Isaac Jr.'s **own will (1702) names "daughter Sarah Lee."** [S — will; Lee Family Archive: "The fifth son of the family founder, Hancock, married (the second time) Sarah Allerton, a granddaughter of Isaac Allerton, who was a passenger on the Mayflower"]

**The relationships, precisely:**
- **Sarah Allerton Lee** descends from Isaac Allerton via his **second** wife Fear Brewster. The user's hypothesized line descends via his **first** wife Mary Norris (through Remember → Maverick → Hawkes). Shared ancestor: **Isaac Allerton only** — half-cousins, several generations removed.
- **Francis Lightfoot Lee (1734–1797)**, signer of the Declaration, was the **great-nephew by marriage** of Sarah: his line runs Richard Lee I → **Richard Lee II** (the scholar, m. Laetitia Corbin) → **Thomas Lee** (president of the King's Council, m. **Hannah Harrison Ludwell**) → Francis Lightfoot. He carried no Allerton blood, and **he died childless** — so "related to Francis Lightfoot Lee" can only ever mean collateral kin, exactly as the tradition states it. [S/B]
- **The blood descendant of the Lee–Allerton marriage is President Zachary Taylor** — great-grandson of Hancock and Sarah — who thereby descends from Isaac Allerton through this Lee line *and* (per the Mayflower Society) through the Maverick line as well. The same marriage later connects to Jefferson Davis through Taylor's daughter. [B — Lee Family FAQ; S — GSMD]
- **The Lee pedigree in England** is among the deepest of any American family: from **Reyner de Lega, c. 1200**, through the Lees of **Coton / Nordley Regis, Shropshire** (Richard Lee, b. 1563, father of the emigrant), per the surviving Cobbs Hall family Bible and the College of Arms, as analyzed by W. L. Montague (*Virginia Magazine of History and Biography*, 1954). If the user's Allerton line proves, this 800-year pedigree enters the family's *collateral* heritage chart.

### 6.1 The children of Hancock Lee and Sarah Allerton [S — James Reid Hancock, "Ann (Lee) Eustace, Daughter of Hancock Lee… and His Second Wife Sarah Allerton," *The Mayflower Descendant* 59 (2010): 13; Merrick, *MD* 42 (1992)]
The modern standard account, which carefully separates Sarah's children from those of Hancock's first wife Mary Kendall:
1. **Anne Lee** (b. ~1700–1702) — m. **William Eustace**. *Not* to be confused with her half-sister **Anna** Lee (daughter of the first marriage), who m. William Armistead — two different daughters, untangled by the Society's researchers.
2. **Isaac Lee** (b. by Jan 1706/7 – d. by Nov 1727) — died young, unmarried ("of Stepney, Middlesex" in one record).
3. **John Lee** (b. by 18 May 1709 – d. 11 Aug 1789) — lived to old age, probably never married.
4. **Elizabeth Lee** (b. 1709 — the **twin sister of Hancock II**) — m. (1) **Swan Jones** (one son, John Jones, b. ~1733); (2), in 1737, **Zachary Taylor** of Orange County, VA. Four Taylor children: Zachary Jr. (b. 1735); Elizabeth (m. Thomas Bell); Hancock Taylor (~1742); and **Richard Taylor (1744–1829)** — Revolutionary colonel, who carried the family to Kentucky and was the **father of President Zachary Taylor**. Elizabeth Lee is thus the president's grandmother, and Sarah Allerton Lee his great-grandmother.
5. **Hancock Lee II** (b. 1709 – d. 1762 per the Society research; some compilations give 1789) — m. **Mary Willis**; of Ditchley and Warrenton; justice of King George County; the son who carried this Lee branch forward.

The couple's tombstones survive at **Ditchley, Northumberland County, VA**, and Sarah's stone states the Mayflower connection outright: *"Sarah, his last Wife, Daughter of Isaac Allerton, Esq., Who departed this Life 17th May, Anno Domo, 1731."* (Hancock's stone styles him the seventh son of Richard Lee the Emigrant.)

*Research footnote — the records stay live:* a 2024 *Mayflower Quarterly* article (Hahn) disproved the old assumption that Sarah had also married a man named Newton; the "Newton" confusion attached to her niece **Mary Allerton Newton** (daughter of Isaac Jr.'s daughter Mary). Sarah's only husband was Hancock Lee.

### 6.2 Robert E. Lee and the Allerton kinship [S/B]
**Robert Edward Lee (1807–1870)** descends: Henry Lee III "Light-Horse Harry" (1756–1818) → Henry Lee II of Leesylvania (1730–1787) → Henry Lee I of Lee Hall (1691–1747) → **Richard Lee II "the Scholar" (1647–1715)** → Richard Lee I the Emigrant. Richard Lee II was the **brother of Hancock Lee** — Sarah Allerton's husband — making Sarah Robert E. Lee's great-great-great-aunt **by marriage**. Like Francis Lightfoot Lee, Robert E. Lee carried **no Allerton blood**; the kinship runs through the marriage, not the bloodstream. The blood relationship inside that family belonged to the user's cousin: **Zachary Taylor was Robert E. Lee's third cousin once removed** (through Richard Lee I's line), and the two served together in the Mexican–American War — Lee on Taylor's staff in northern Mexico. *(Flagged, unproven, logged: Robert E. Lee's wife, Mary Anna Randolph Custis, descends from the Virginia Parke–Custis line — Frances Parke, daughter of Governor Daniel Parke, married John Custis IV of Williamsburg; that Parke family is a different immigrant line from the user's Hopewell Parkes, §3.2.)*

**Why the tradition is trustworthy:** the family's three Allerton traditions (via Carpenter/Hawkes; kin to the Lees; the names Fear Brewster and Sarah) are independent memories that all converge on the same ancestor — the signature of genuine transmission rather than borrowed glamour.

### 7.11 The Noah correction — stated once, plainly
Noah Webster (1758–1843) descends: Gov. John → **Robert → John (b. 1653, m. Sarah Mygatt) → Deacon/Captain Daniel Webster (m. Miriam Cooke, widow Kellogg) → Noah Webster Sr. (1722–1813, farmer, deacon, militia captain, justice of the peace; m. Mercy Steele 1749) → Noah Jr.**
The Abbott line descends: Gov. John → **Robert → Jonathan → Benjamin → … → Guy → Louise → Abbott.**
Both lines share **Robert**; they split at Robert's sons. **The user and Noah Webster Jr. are both 7th-generation descendants of Governor John Webster — distant cousins.** The family's tradition "direct to Noah" is thus a compression of a true statement: *direct to Governor John Webster — the same ancestor Noah had.* The middle-name tradition ("Webster" across four Abbott generations) is how a *cousin-branch* keeps the famous name alive.
*(For completeness: Noah Jr. had a brother **Abraham Kellogg Webster** whose son Frederick settled at Whitestown, Oneida Co., NY — the same county as the Abbotts; and Noah's son **Richard Webster** of Mauch Chunk, PA, appears in an 1848 letter. The historian **Paul Leicester Ford** was Noah's great-grandson through Noah's daughter Emily.)*

### 8.3 The kin-web (expanded, with relationship math and honest tags)

## 9. The Hannay Thread — Clan Hannay of Sorbie

**Family tradition:** "Our relatives were the Hanna clan of the Sorbie castle." — identified as **Clan Hannay of Sorbie Tower** ("the Old Place of Sorbie"), Wigtownshire, Galloway, Scotland.

**Documented clan history [A/B — clan records, published pedigrees]:**
- The name appears on the **Ragman Roll of 1296** (Gilbert de Hannethe submitted to Edward I); the clan's sway over the Machars of Galloway was such that the region was called **"Machars Hannay."**
- **Sorbie**: Odo Hannay, first recorded owner (mid-1400s) → Robert (sheriff-depute of Wigtownshire, 1498) → Patrick (murdered 1543) → **Alexander A'hanna**, who built the surviving tower house c. 1550–1580 while feuding with the Murrays of Broughton, the Kennedys, and the Stewarts.

Published pedigrees: ***Hanna of Castle Sorbie, Scotland, and Descendants*** and ***The House of Hanna*** — both free on archive.org; the latter opens: *"All bearing the name are undoubtedly to be traced to a lowland clan of Scotland, whose chief had his residence in Sorbie Castle."*

Kinnersly, Jan. 30, 1818"** — documented as bound into (1) the Brightly & Kinnersley *Most Superb Folio and Self-Interpreting Bible* (New York, 1813; plates 1812–1818; issued in 162 monthly parts; tall thick folio), (2) Kinnersley's 1822 stereotyped edition (the first stereotyped Bible printed in the US), and (3) the **Isaac Collins-lineage quartos** — including a printing **sold at Auburn and Buffalo, NY (John E. Beardsley, 1820s–30s)**, squarely in the family's western-New-York corridor. The plate's own publication line (bottom margin) identifies it from one photograph. A hand-colored Babel *frontispiece* is documented in the thick quartos of the **Isaac Collins stereotype lineage** (Trenton 1791; reprinted by Holbrook at Brattleborough VT 1816/1817/1821, Andrus at Hartford, Carey at Philadelphia — the standard American family Bible of 1810–1840, often "with Canne's Marginal Notes" and Brown's Concordance), while steel-engraved Babel plates sit among the 20–40 plates of the mid-century illustrated family Bibles (Fanshaw/Butler/Virtue; the 1867 Virtue, Yorkston & Co. *Devotional Family Bible* is a documented example). A Collins-lineage quarto would fit the family's Massachusetts years (acquired before the Petersham→Oswego migration); a cloth-bound illustrated Bible fits an Oswego purchase of 1845–1875. Edition identification requires only two photographs: the **title page** and the **frontispiece/plate list**; dating tells: steel engravings → 1840s–60s, Doré-style illustrations → 1866+, color plates → 1870s+. Capture protocol: title page → every family-register page (straight-on, both sides; note changes of handwriting) → all flyleaf inscriptions ("Presented to…" entries often name the parent-to-child hand-off) → frontispiece and plate list → covers/spine under angled light. If the Bible's Moses Hawkes matches **Moses Hawks b. 1779, Petersham** (Daniel Hawkes 1749's documented son), the artifact itself closes the Allerton line.
